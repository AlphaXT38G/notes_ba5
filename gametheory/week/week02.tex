\section{Recap: the Battle of the Sexes}


This game summarizes the techniques of the previous lecture: how to find a pure-strategy NE,
how to find a mixed-strategy NE, and that the same game can have both types of NE.


\begin{example}

\textbf{Story}

Two friends prefer to go out together than to go out alone. But Adam prefers to go to a
soccer game and Bob prefers to go to the opera. They don't have phones so cannot
communicate before deciding where to go.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{c|cc}
Adam $\backslash$ Bob & Soccer & Opera \\
\hline
Soccer & (2,1) & (0,0) \\
Opera & (0,0) & (1,2) \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\end{example}


\textbf{Pure-strategy NE}

\begin{itemize}

\item If Adam goes to soccer, also Bob prefers to go to soccer. If Adam goes to the opera,
also Bob prefers to go to the opera.

\item If Bob goes to soccer, also Adam prefers to go to soccer. If Bob goes to the opera,
also Adam prefers to go to the opera.

\end{itemize}


\begin{important}

\textbf{Soccer-Soccer} and \textbf{Opera-Opera} are the two pure-strategy NE.

\end{important}

\section{Mixed-Strategy Nash Equilibria}


\begin{important}

In a game with $N$ actions, to adopt a mixed strategy
$s^{(i)} = (p^{(1)}, p^{(2)}, \dots, p^{(N)})$, a player must be \textbf{indifferent
between all the actions to which the strategy assigns non-zero probability}.

\textbf{Why?} Because if one of these actions gave lower expected payout than the other
actions, the player would never adopt it.

\end{important}


This is the principle that guides us to find mixed-strategy Nash Equilibria.


\begin{formula}

\textbf{Bob indifferent}: Adam plays $s^{(A)} = (p_S^{(A)}, 1 - p_S^{(A)})$.

\begin{itemize}

\item Bob goes to soccer:
$p_S^{(A)} \times 1 + (1 - p_S^{(A)}) \times 0 = p_S^{(A)}$

\item Bob goes to the opera:
$p_S^{(A)} \times 0 + (1 - p_S^{(A)}) \times 2 = 2 - 2p_S^{(A)}$

\end{itemize}

\[
\Rightarrow \quad p_S^{(A)} = 2/3, \quad s^{(A)} = (2/3, 1/3)
\]

\textbf{Adam indifferent}: Bob plays $s^{(B)} = (p_S^{(B)}, 1 - p_S^{(B)})$.

\begin{itemize}

\item Adam goes to soccer:
$p_S^{(B)} \times 2 + (1 - p_S^{(B)}) \times 0 = 2p_S^{(B)}$

\item Adam goes to the opera:
$p_S^{(B)} \times 0 + (1 - p_S^{(B)}) \times 1 = 1 - p_S^{(B)}$

\end{itemize}

\[
\Rightarrow \quad p_S^{(B)} = 1/3, \quad s^{(B)} = (1/3, 2/3)
\]

\end{formula}


\begin{important}

\textbf{Mixed-strategy Nash equilibrium}:
\[
s^{(A)} = (2/3, 1/3), \qquad s^{(B)} = (1/3, 2/3)
\]

\end{important}

\section{Beauty Contest Game}


\begin{definition}

\textbf{BEAUTY CONTEST GAME}

\begin{itemize}

\item $N$ players, e.g.\ all the students in a class.

\item Each has to pick a number between 1 and 100.

\item The player whose number is closest to \textbf{2/3 of the average of all the numbers}
wins 1.

\item If $m > 1$ players pick the same number, which is the closest to 2/3 of the average,
they split the gain.

\end{itemize}

\end{definition}


\begin{example}

\textbf{Concept introduced by Keynes}

How do stock traders choose which stocks to buy and sell? Not by naively thinking about their
fundamental value, but by trying to guess how other people guess their value, which in turn
will be based on how they think other people guess their value\dots

Keynes' analogy: a newspaper contest, in which entrants choose the six most attractive faces
from a hundred photographs; those who picked the most popular faces are eligible for a prize.
The point is not to pick the faces you find the prettiest, nor those average opinion finds
the prettiest, but to anticipate what average opinion expects average opinion to be (the
``third degree''), and some go to the fourth, fifth and higher degrees (Keynes,
\emph{General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money}, 1936).

\end{example}


\separator


\textbf{What should you have played?}

\begin{itemize}

\item \textbf{\#1} Never play a number $> 67$: even if all the others choose 100, 2/3 of the
average will be $< 68$.

\item \textbf{\#2} If everybody else understood \#1, the highest they will ever play is 67.
So never play a number $> 45$ (even if all the others play 67, 2/3 of the average would be
$< 45$).

\item \textbf{\#3} If everybody else understood \#2 (everybody understood that everybody
understood \#1), the highest they will ever play is 45: never choose a number $> 30$.

\item \textbf{\#4} If everybody else understood \#3: never play a number $> 20$.

\item \textbf{\#5} \dots\ $> 13$, \textbf{\#6} \dots\ $> 9$, \textbf{\#7} \dots\ $> 6$,
\textbf{\#8} \dots\ $> 4$, \textbf{\#9} \dots\ $> 3$, \textbf{\#10} \dots\ $> 2$,
\textbf{\#11} \dots\ $> 1$.

\end{itemize}


\begin{important}

After 11 levels of reasoning: \textbf{you should play 1!!}

\textbf{The unique Nash equilibrium is: everybody plays 1 and everybody shares the prize.}

\end{important}


\begin{warning}

Most lab experiments find that the winning number is usually between 10 and 20: people don't
go through the 11 levels of reasoning and instead stop at level 4-5\dots

If other people do not follow the reasoning, playing 1 is not the best strategy\dots

\end{warning}

\section{Dominant Strategies}


\begin{definition}

\textbf{STRICTLY DOMINANT STRATEGY}

For player $i$, a strategy $\tilde{s}^{(i)}$ is strictly dominant if it results in a
strictly higher payout for player $i$ than any other strategy, \emph{no matter what strategy
the other players adopt}:

\[
\mathcal{P}^{(i)}(\tilde{s}^{(i)}, s^{(-i)}) > \mathcal{P}^{(i)}(s^{(i)}, s^{(-i)}),
\quad \forall s^{(-i)}, \ \forall s^{(i)} \ne \tilde{s}^{(i)}
\]

\end{definition}


\begin{definition}

\textbf{WEAKLY DOMINANT STRATEGY}

For player $i$, a strategy $\tilde{s}^{(i)}$ is weakly dominant if it results in a weakly
higher payout for player $i$ than any other strategy, \emph{no matter what strategy the
other players adopt}:

\[
\mathcal{P}^{(i)}(\tilde{s}^{(i)}, s^{(-i)}) \ge \mathcal{P}^{(i)}(s^{(i)}, s^{(-i)}),
\quad \forall s^{(-i)}, \ \forall s^{(i)}
\]

\end{definition}


\begin{example}

\textbf{Prisoner Dilemma}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{c|cc}
 & C & D \\
\hline
C & (-1,-1) & (-5,0) \\
D & (0,-5) & (-3,-3) \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

D is \textbf{strictly dominant} for both players ($0 > -1$ and $-3 > -5$).

\end{example}


\begin{example}

\textbf{Split or Steal}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{c|cc}
 & Split & Steal \\
\hline
Split & (1,1) & (0,2) \\
Steal & (2,0) & (0,0) \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

No strictly dominant strategy. Steal is \textbf{weakly dominant} ($2 > 1$ against Split,
$0 = 0$ against Steal).

\end{example}

\section{Dominated Strategies}


\begin{definition}

\textbf{STRICTLY DOMINATED STRATEGY}

For player $i$, a strategy $\bar{s}^{(i)}$ is strictly dominated if, for any strategy
adopted by the other players, there is at least another strategy $\tilde{s}^{(i)}$ that
gives a strictly higher payout for Player $i$:

\[
\forall s^{(-i)} \ \exists \tilde{s}^{(i)} \ s.t. \
\mathcal{P}^{(i)}(\tilde{s}^{(i)}, s^{(-i)}) > \mathcal{P}^{(i)}(\bar{s}^{(i)}, s^{(-i)})
\]

\end{definition}


\begin{definition}

\textbf{WEAKLY DOMINATED STRATEGY}

$\bar{s}^{(i)}$ is weakly dominated iff

\[
\forall s^{(-i)} \ \exists \tilde{s}^{(i)} \ s.t. \
\mathcal{P}^{(i)}(\tilde{s}^{(i)}, s^{(-i)}) \ge \mathcal{P}^{(i)}(\bar{s}^{(i)}, s^{(-i)})
\]

\end{definition}


\begin{example}

\textbf{Example 1} -- Is $R$ a dominated strategy for Player 2?

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{c|ccc}
 & L & M & R \\
\hline
U & $(x_1,0)$ & $(x_2,3)$ & $(x_3,1)$ \\
D & $(x_4,3)$ & $(x_5,0)$ & $(x_6,1)$ \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The most general strategy for Player 1 is $s^{(1)} = (p_U^{(1)}, 1 - p_U^{(1)})$.

\begin{itemize}

\item If $p_U > 1/3$, strategy $R$ is dominated by strategy $M$.

\item If $p_U < 2/3$, strategy $R$ is dominated by strategy $L$.

\end{itemize}

$\rightarrow$ Strategy $R$ is \textbf{strictly dominated}.

\end{example}


\begin{example}

\textbf{Example 2} -- Is $R$ a dominated strategy for Player 2?

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{c|ccc}
 & L & M & R \\
\hline
U & $(x_1,0)$ & $(x_2,3)$ & $(x_3,2)$ \\
D & $(x_4,3)$ & $(x_5,0)$ & $(x_6,2)$ \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{itemize}

\item If $p_U > 2/3$, strategy $R$ is dominated by strategy $M$.

\item If $p_U < 1/3$, strategy $R$ is dominated by strategy $L$.

\item However, for $1/3 \le p_U \le 2/3$ strategy $R$ is an optimal strategy (strictly the
best for $1/3 < p_U < 2/3$).

\end{itemize}

$\rightarrow$ Strategy $R$ is \textbf{not a dominated strategy}.

\end{example}

\section{Iterated Elimination of Dominated Strategies}


\begin{definition}

\textbf{ITERATED ELIMINATION OF DOMINATED STRATEGIES}

A \textbf{solution concept} that allows to simplify (sometimes solve) a game.

Idea: if a strategy is (strictly) dominated, I should never choose it. The game should be
equivalent if I discard this strategy altogether.

\end{definition}


\begin{example}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{c|ccc}
 & L & M & R \\
\hline
U & (1,0) & (1,2) & (0,1) \\
D & (0,3) & (0,1) & (2,0) \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{Step 1}: for Player 2, strategy $R$ is dominated by strategy $M$ $\rightarrow$ we
can eliminate strategy $R$:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{c|cc}
 & L & M \\
\hline
U & (1,0) & (1,2) \\
D & (0,3) & (0,1) \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{Step 2}: given that $R$ was discarded, for Player 1 strategy $D$ is dominated by
strategy $U$:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{c|cc}
 & L & M \\
\hline
U & (1,0) & (1,2) \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The game is greatly simplified: the NE is clearly $(U,M)$ (Player 1 plays $U$, Player 2
plays $M$).

\end{example}



%=========================================================
\section{Theorem -- Strict Elimination}


\begin{important}

Given an $N$-player game $G$, call $G_1$ the game obtained from $G$ after iterated
elimination of one or more \textbf{strictly} dominated strategies.

\begin{itemize}

\item If the strategy profile $(s^{(1)}, s^{(2)}, \dots, s^{(N)})$ is a Nash equilibrium of
$G$, it is a Nash equilibrium of $G_1$.

\item If $G$ is finite and $(s^{(1)}, s^{(2)}, \dots, s^{(N)})$ is a Nash equilibrium of
$G_1$, it is a Nash equilibrium of $G$.

\end{itemize}

\end{important}



%=========================================================
\section{Iterated Elimination of Weakly Dominated Strategies}


\begin{warning}

In the Split or Steal game, we could eliminate the weakly dominated Split strategy. However,
only one out of the three equilibria, \textbf{(Steal, Steal)}, would be preserved!

\end{warning}


\begin{important}

\textbf{Theorem (weak)}

Consider a finite game $G$ and a game $G_1$ obtained from $G$ after iterated elimination of
\textbf{weakly} dominated strategies. If $(s^{(1)}, s^{(2)}, \dots, s^{(N)})$ is a Nash
equilibrium of $G_1$, it is a Nash equilibrium of $G$.

\end{important}


\begin{warning}

\begin{itemize}

\item Only some Nash Equilibria are preserved by the iterated elimination of weakly
dominated strategies.

\item Which equilibria are preserved \textbf{can depend on the order of elimination}.

\end{itemize}

\end{warning}


\begin{example}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{c|cc}
 & L & R \\
\hline
U & (5,1) & (4,0) \\
M & (6,0) & (3,1) \\
D & (6,4) & (4,4) \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Two NE: $(D,L)$ and $(D,R)$.

\textbf{Solution 1}

\begin{itemize}

\item Player 1 eliminates $M$, weakly dominated by $D$.

\item Player 2 eliminates $R$, weakly dominated by $L$.

\item The game becomes $U$: (5,1), $D$: (6,4) in column $L$: the unique NE is
$\boldsymbol{(D,L)}$.

\end{itemize}

\textbf{Solution 2}

\begin{itemize}

\item Player 1 eliminates $U$, weakly dominated by $D$.

\item Player 2 eliminates $L$, weakly dominated by $R$.

\item The game becomes $M$: (3,1), $D$: (4,4) in column $R$: the unique NE is
$\boldsymbol{(D,R)}$.

\end{itemize}

\end{example}



%=========================================================
\section{Beauty Contest and Iterated Elimination}


\begin{important}

The 11 levels of reasoning can be interpreted as \textbf{Iterated Elimination of (Weakly)
Dominated Strategies}.

\begin{itemize}

\item Everybody eliminates the choices $> 67$, which are weakly dominated.

\item After this elimination, the choices $> 45$ become weakly dominated, so we can
eliminate them\dots

\item In the end, we find the Nash equilibrium in which everybody plays 1, and everybody
gets $1/N$ of the prize.

\end{itemize}

\end{important}


\begin{warning}

Iterated deletion of weakly dominated strategies may lose some equilibria, so there could be
other Nash equilibria of this game; but it can be proved that this is the only one.

\end{warning}

\section{Weekly Summary}


\begin{summary}

\begin{itemize}

\item A game can have both pure- and mixed-strategy NE (Battle of the Sexes).

\item To randomize, a player must be indifferent between all the actions she plays with
non-zero probability.

\item Beauty Contest: unique NE in which everybody plays 1.

\item Strictly / weakly \textbf{dominant} and \textbf{dominated} strategies.

\item Iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies keeps all the NE (finite game).

\item Iterated elimination of weakly dominated strategies can lose NE, and the result can
depend on the order of elimination.

\end{itemize}

\end{summary}
