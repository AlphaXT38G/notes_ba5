\section{The Braess Paradox}


\begin{definition}

\textbf{BRAESS PARADOX}

$N$-player, simultaneous-move game (complete information) modeling a transportation network.

Paradox: \textbf{adding a new, very fast route to a transportation network can increase
traffic and transportation time for everybody}.

\end{definition}


\begin{example}

\textbf{Real-world cases}

\begin{itemize}

\item \textbf{Stuttgart, 1969}: shortly after D.\ Braess published his theory, the city
invested heavily in its downtown road network by building a highly efficient new road
segment. Traffic immediately ground to a halt. When the city closed the newly built road
section, traffic immediately returned to normal.

\item \textbf{NYC, 1990}: 42nd street (one of Manhattan's busiest crosstown arteries) closed
on ``Earth day''. Media outlets warned of a ``traffic apocalypse'', but when the street
closed, the surrounding gridlock dropped drastically, and traffic moved faster.

\item \textbf{Seoul, South Korea, 2003}: the destruction of a six-lane highway to build a
park actually improved travel time into and out of the city. Traffic volume stayed roughly
the same before and after.

\end{itemize}

\end{example}

\section{Braess -- Initial Situation}


\begin{center}

\includegraphics[
width=0.7\linewidth
]{../images/week05_01.png}

\end{center}


\begin{itemize}

\item \textbf{N = 4000 cars} (players). Two \textbf{directed} routes to go from A to B.

\item Travel time on an ``edge'' of the graph (e.g.\ edge AC) is a function of the number of
cars: $T_e = T_e(x)$.

\item Payout for each car is $-T_e(x)$: each car's objective is to \textbf{minimize} travel
time.

\end{itemize}


\textbf{Is there a NE in which all cars take the same route?} No. If all cars went through C
(route AC+CB), travel time would be $4000/100 + 45 = 85$ minutes. A car would have an
incentive to switch to the route through D (AD+DB): $1/100 + 45 = 45.01$.


\begin{important}

For different cars to take different routes (still a \textbf{pure-strategy NE}), since all
cars are identical, with the same payout, travel time should be the same on the two routes
\emph{in the limit $N \to \infty$, in which a single car cannot affect the travel time of a
route}.

\textbf{2000 cars on each route is a NE}, with travel time $2000/100 + 45 = 65$ minutes for
each car.

\end{important}


\begin{example}

\textbf{Odd number of cars}: e.g.\ 7 cars, 3 on AC+CB and 4 on AD+DB. Is this a NE? Yes,
because even for the cars on AD+DB, switching to AC+CB would leave travel time unchanged.

\end{example}

\section{Braess -- New Route is Added}


\begin{center}

\includegraphics[
width=0.7\linewidth
]{../images/week05_02.png}

\end{center}


New, superfast edge CD (can only go from C to D). Three possible routes from A to B now:
AC+CB; AD+DB; AC+CD+DB.


\begin{itemize}

\item As before, everybody taking AC+CB, or everybody taking AD+DB, cannot be a NE.

\item \textbf{Everybody on AC+CD+DB}: travel time $= 4000/100 + 0 + 4000/100 = 80$. If somebody
switched to AC+CB, travel time would be $4000/100 + 45 = 85$; the same if somebody switched
to AD+DB. So \textbf{AC+CD+DB is a NE!}

\item \textbf{Half on AC+CB, half on AD+DB} (65 minutes) is not a NE anymore: if one car
switches to AC+CD+DB, travel time would be $2001/100 + 0 + 1999/100 \simeq 40$ minutes.

\end{itemize}


\begin{important}

No matter the strategy of the other players, switching from AC+CD+DB to another route involves
switching from an edge with travel time $x/100$ (at most 40) to an edge with travel time 45.

\textbf{AC+CD+DB is the only NE!} Travel time is 80, \textbf{15 minutes longer} than the NE
before the route was added.

\end{important}

\section{A Pure-Strategy NE for Every Network}


\begin{definition}

\textbf{POTENTIAL ENERGY}

Potential energy of an edge $e$ when $x_e$ cars travel on this edge:

\[
E_e(x_e) = T_e(1) + T_e(2) + \dots + T_e(x_e)
\]

Potential energy of a network $\mathcal{N}$: the sum of the potential energy of all its
edges:

\[
E_{\mathcal{N}} = \sum_{e \in \mathcal{N}} E_e(x_e)
\]

\end{definition}


\begin{warning}

Potential energy is different from the aggregate travel time (total time spent on this edge
by the cars traveling on it), which is $x\,T_e(x)$.

\end{warning}


\begin{important}

\textbf{If one single car leaves the network, potential energy decreases by exactly the
traveling time of this car.}

\end{important}


\begin{example}

3 cars, one on AC+CB and 2 on AD+DB:

\begin{align*}
E_{\mathcal{N}} ={}& T_{AC}(1) + T_{CB}(1) \\
&+ (T_{AD}(1) + T_{AD}(2)) + (T_{DB}(1) + T_{DB}(2))
\end{align*}

One car traveling on AD+DB leaves the network:

\[
E_{\mathcal{N}} = T_{AC}(1) + T_{CB}(1) + T_{AD}(1) + T_{DB}(1)
\]

The difference is $T_{AD}(2) + T_{DB}(2)$: the traveling time of this car.

If this car re-enters on another route, e.g.\ AC+CB, the potential energy increases by its
traveling time on the new route, $T_{AC}(2) + T_{CB}(2)$:

\begin{align*}
E_{\mathcal{N}} ={}& (T_{AC}(1) + T_{AC}(2)) + (T_{CB}(1) + T_{CB}(2)) \\
&+ T_{AD}(1) + T_{DB}(1)
\end{align*}

\end{example}


\begin{important}

\begin{itemize}

\item When one car switches to a better route (which decreases its traveling time),
\textbf{potential energy decreases}.

\item (Pure-strategy) Nash equilibrium corresponds to a \textbf{min of potential energy}.

\item Only a finite number of configurations $\rightarrow$ there is necessarily a min of
potential energy. So \textbf{there is necessarily a pure-strategy NE}.

\end{itemize}

\end{important}

\section{Finding a NE for an Arbitrary Network}


\begin{summary}

\textbf{Pseudocode}

\begin{enumerate}

\item Start from an arbitrary configuration (strategy profile), compute potential energy.

\item Consider all possible changes of strategy \textbf{for 1 player}. If there is any change
that decreases potential energy, update the configuration.

\item Stop when no favorable change is found. This is the NE and the minimum of Potential
energy.

\end{enumerate}

\end{summary}

\section{Recap: Bayesian Games}


\begin{definition}

\textbf{BAYESIAN GAME} (simultaneous-move game of incomplete information)

Defined by: players, an action set for each player, possible types $\theta_i$ for each player
$i$ and a probability distribution over player $i$ types $p(\theta_i)$.

A pure strategy is a plan of action \textbf{for each type of player}.

\end{definition}


\begin{definition}

\textbf{BAYESIAN NASH EQUILIBRIUM (BNE)}

$(s_1(\theta_1), \dots, s_N(\theta_N))$ is a BNE iff, for each player $i$, her
\textbf{expected payout} is higher than or equal to her expected payout using any of the other
available strategies, keeping fixed the strategies of the other players, which \textbf{depend
on their types}:

\[
\mathcal{P}_i(s_i(\theta_i), s_{-i}(\theta_{-i}) | \theta_i) \ge
\mathcal{P}_i(\tilde{s}_i(\theta_i), s_{-i}(\theta_{-i}) | \theta_i)
\]

Take the expected value over all possible types of the other players.

\end{definition}


\begin{example}

\textbf{Public good and free riding} (see week 04)

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{c|cc}
$1 \backslash 2$ & W & N \\
\hline
W & $(1-c_1, 1-c_2)$ & $(1-c_1, 1)$ \\
N & $(1, 1-c_2)$ & $(0,0)$ \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

$c_1 = 0.5$ known to everybody; $c_2 = 0.2$ (Efficient type, prob $p$) or $1.2$ (Inefficient
type, prob $1-p$), known only to Player 2; $p$ is common knowledge. `Work' is dominated for
the Inefficient type.

\begin{itemize}

\item Efficient works, Inefficient does not: Player 1 gets 0.5 if he works, $p$ if he does
not $\rightarrow$ works if $p < 0.5$, does not work if $p > 0.5$. For $p > 0.5$:
\textbf{BNE $(N, WN)$}.

\item Player 2 never works: Player 1 works regardless of $p$: \textbf{BNE $(W, NN)$}.

\end{itemize}

\end{example}


\begin{important}

\begin{itemize}

\item If $p < 0.5$: unique pure-strategy BNE $(W, NN)$.

\item If $p > 0.5$: two pure-strategy BNE $(W, NN)$ and $(N, WN)$.

\end{itemize}

\end{important}


\textbf{Harsanyi representation} (introduced in the last lecture): one table with the
strategy of Player 2 conditional on his type (`WN' = ``Work if efficient, Not Work if
Inefficient'').

\begin{center}
\footnotesize
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{3pt}
\begin{tabular}{c|cccc}
$1 \backslash 2$ & WN & NN & NW & WW \\
\hline
W & (0.5, 1-0.2p) & (0.5, 1) & (0.5, 1.2p-0.2) & (0.5, p-0.2) \\
N & (p, 0.8p) & (0,0) & (1-p, -0.2(1-p)) & (1, p-0.2) \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

After elimination of the dominated strategy W for the Inefficient Player 2, only WN and NN
remain: we find the same pure-strategy equilibria, $(W, NN)$ if $p < 0.5$, $(W, NN)$ and
$(N, WN)$ for $p > 0.5$.

\section{Public Good with a Continuum of Types}


Same game, but $c_1$ and $c_2$ can take a \textbf{continuum of values}: for example $c_1$ and
$c_2$ \textbf{uniformly distributed in the interval $[0, 2]$}.


\begin{formula}

The probability that each of these variables is between $x_1$ and $x_2 \in [0,2]$ is the
length of the interval $|x_1 - x_2|$ divided by the total length of the domain (here 2).

\end{formula}


\begin{center}

\includegraphics[
width=0.9\linewidth
]{../images/week05_03.png}

\end{center}


\begin{itemize}

\item Reasonable to expect that when the cost of Player $i$ is low, she will decide to work;
when her cost is high, she will not work.

\item No player would ever work if her cost is above 1.

\item Look for a solution where each player works if her cost is below a \textbf{threshold
$T_i^*$} (not necessarily the same for the two players).

\end{itemize}


\begin{formula}

Player 2's threshold is $T_2^*$. What should Player 1 do?

\begin{itemize}

\item If she works, her payout is

\[
\mathcal{P}_1^W = 1 - c_1
\]

\item If she does not work, her expected payout is 1 times the probability that Player 2
works, i.e.\ the probability that $c_2 < T_2^*$:

\[
\mathcal{P}_1^N = \frac{T_2^*}{2}
\]

\end{itemize}

Player 1 works if $\mathcal{P}_1^W > \mathcal{P}_1^N$, hence her threshold is

\[
T_1^* = 1 - \frac{T_2^*}{2}
\]

The same way, Player 2's best response to $T_1^*$ is

\[
T_2^* = 1 - \frac{T_1^*}{2}
\]

\end{formula}


\begin{important}

The solution is

\[
T_1^* = T_2^* = \frac{2}{3}
\]

\end{important}

\section{Challenge: Public Good Two Days in a Row}


\begin{example}

What if we played twice the same game?

\begin{itemize}

\item Each day, each player decides whether or not to work. Same payout matrix.

\item The costs $c_1$ and $c_2$ are known only to the respective player. However, each
player's cost is \textbf{THE SAME ON BOTH DAYS}.

\end{itemize}

\end{example}


\textbf{Would you use the same strategy on both days?}

On day 2, you have more information. If my ``opponent'' worked on day 1, her cost is likely to
be low $\rightarrow$ she is also more likely to work on day 2 $\rightarrow$ my threshold for
working is lower\dots


\begin{warning}

On day one, would you behave the same as in the 1-period game?

\end{warning}
