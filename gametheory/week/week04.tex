\section{The Pirate Game}


\begin{example}

\textbf{Rules} -- 5 pirates A, B, C, D, E share 100 coins.

\begin{itemize}

\item Captain A proposes how to split the 100 coins. If at least \textbf{half} (3 in this
case, including himself) the pirates vote yes, the vote passes.

\item If the vote does not pass, A is killed and B becomes captain, and makes a new proposal,
that needs to be voted by at least half (2 in this case) of the remaining pirates, otherwise B
is killed and C becomes captain\dots

\item A pirate needs to be \textbf{strictly better off} with the proposed plan than in the
continuation of the game to vote yes.

\end{itemize}

What's the Subgame-Perfect equilibrium? Will captain Amaro be thrown in the ocean? If not,
how many of the 100 coins will he be able to keep?

\end{example}


\textbf{Pirates left at each time}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ccccc}
t=1 & t=2 & t=3 & t=4 & t=5 \\
\hline
A & B & C & D & E \\
B & C & D & E & \\
C & D & E & & \\
D & E & & & \\
E & & & & \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}


\begin{formula}

\textbf{Backward induction} (coins of A, B, C, D, E; ``--'' = already killed)

\begin{itemize}

\item \textbf{t=4} (D, E): D's own vote is half of 2: D keeps everything.
\hfill (--, --, --, 100, 0)

\item \textbf{t=3} (C, D, E): C needs 2 votes. E gets 0 at t=4, so 1 coin makes him strictly
better off. \hfill (--, --, 99, 0, 1)

\item \textbf{t=2} (B, C, D, E): B needs 2 votes. D gets 0 at t=3: 1 coin buys his vote.
\hfill (--, 99, 0, 1, 0)

\item \textbf{t=1} (A, \dots, E): A needs 3 votes. C and E get 0 at t=2: 1 coin each.
\hfill (98, 0, 1, 0, 1)

\end{itemize}

\end{formula}


\begin{important}

\textbf{SPE}: captain A proposes $(98, 0, 1, 0, 1)$; A, C and E vote yes. He is not thrown
in the ocean, and keeps \textbf{98 coins}.

\end{important}

\section{The Centipede Game}


\begin{center}

\includegraphics[
width=\linewidth
]{../images/week04_01.png}

\end{center}


\begin{example}

\begin{itemize}

\item Players: 1 and 2, moving in turn: Continue (C) or Stop (S).

\item In each period, the total payout (sum of the payouts of Player 1 and Player 2)
increases by 2 units. One would think players would want to continue playing as long as
possible.

\end{itemize}

\end{example}


\begin{important}

\textbf{Backward induction}

\begin{itemize}

\item At the last node, it's optimal for Player 2 to stop: (99,101) instead of (102,100).

\item At each node, knowing that at the next node the other player will stop, the player who
moves at that time finds it optimal to stop.

\item The unique SPE involves Player 1 stopping at the first node, with payout
\textbf{(2,0)}: each player quits at every node.

\end{itemize}

Lost opportunity to gain a total of up to 202 for the players! Is backward induction
realistic?

\end{important}


\begin{example}

\textbf{The Centipede game in the lab}

\begin{itemize}

\item People asked to play this game typically do not quit in the first stages.

\item Interpretation: people have a higher tendency to collaborate than predicted by the
model. They understand that if they both continue, their payout will increase over time, so
they take the risk to continue, which pays off if also the other player continues.

\item Different from the Prisoner Dilemma: one player has an incentive to cooperate if also
the other player cooperates.

\end{itemize}

\end{example}

\section{The Dollar Auction Game}


How much would you pay to get one dollar? When people play this game in lab experiments,
they end up paying much more than a dollar!


\begin{center}

\includegraphics[
width=0.6\linewidth
]{../images/week04_02.png}

\end{center}


\begin{definition}

\textbf{DOLLAR AUCTION -- FINITE-HORIZON VERSION}

\begin{itemize}

\item Players: 1 and 2.

\item Player 1 starts and can decide to bid or stay out. If she stays out, the game is over
and everybody gets 0. Otherwise she bids 10 cents.

\item Then at each period, the player that did not play last period can decide to quit or
outbid the other player by 5 cents.

\item When a player quits, the other player gets the dollar, but \textbf{both players need
to pay their highest bid}.

\item If no player quit before, the game ends when one player's bid reaches \$10.

\end{itemize}

\end{definition}


\begin{center}

\includegraphics[
width=\linewidth
]{../images/week04_03.png}

\end{center}


\begin{important}

The longer the game continues, the worse off is the total payout.

\end{important}


\textbf{Backward induction} (from the end):

\begin{itemize}

\item \textbf{T=199} (last period): Player 1 is sure to get the dollar if he bids, so by
bidding he gains \$0.90 for sure (relative to not bidding).

\item \textbf{T=198}: Player 2 quits for sure: if he bids, in the last period Player 1 will
bid again for sure and get the dollar. By bidding, Player 2 loses \$0.10 for sure (relative
to not bidding).

\item \textbf{T=197}: Player 1 bids for sure, because, since Player 2 quits for sure at
T=198, by bidding at T=197 Player 1 gains \$0.90 for sure.

\item \textbf{T=196}: Player 2 quits for sure, because at T=197 Player 1 bids for sure
$\rightarrow$ by quitting, Player 2 saves \$0.10 relative to bidding.

\item \dots\ Same reasoning until the beginning of the game.

\end{itemize}


\begin{important}

\textbf{SPE}: Player 1 bids in every one of his decision nodes, Player 2 quits in every one of
his decision nodes.

Player 1 bids \$0.10 at T=1, Player 2 quits at T=2: the payout is \textbf{(0.90, 0)}.

\end{important}


\separator


\textbf{New version}: the game ends when a bid \emph{exceeds} \$10. Then the last player (if
nobody quits before) would be Player 2, when he bids \$10.05.


\begin{important}

\begin{itemize}

\item From the end: Player 2 will continue in the last period (T=200), so Player 1 will quit
at T=199, so Player 2 will continue at T=198\dots

\item SPE: Player 2, with the advantage of being the last if nobody quits before, will always
continue, and Player 1 will always quit.

\item But since Player 1 starts, he will not even enter the game: the payout is
\textbf{(0,0)}.

\end{itemize}

\end{important}

\section{Dollar Auction: Infinite-Horizon Version}


In the finite game, one player is sure to be the one that bids in the ``final period'' (when
bids reach or exceed the \$10 threshold), so he is sure to win if nobody quits. The other
player is sure to lose: he stays out from the beginning.

What if there is no final time and the auction continues until somebody drops out? (Player 1
moves in every odd period, Player 2 in every even period.)


\begin{important}

If nobody quits between a certain time $t$ and $t+2$, \textbf{nothing has changed between $t$
and $t+2$}, except the fact that both players have already committed to pay 10c more than at
period $t$.

But this is a \textbf{sunk cost} that should not affect the continuation of the game!

\end{important}


\textbf{Stationary strategies}: each player quits or continues with the same probability at
each of her decision nodes. At a node where Player $i$ has already committed to pay $x$:


\begin{formula}

If she continues:

\[
P_i(x) =
\begin{cases}
-0.1 + P_i(x) & \text{if Player } -i \text{ continues} \\
-x + 0.9 & \text{if Player } -i \text{ quits}
\end{cases}
\]

If she quits:

\[
P_i(x) = -x
\]

\end{formula}


\begin{itemize}

\item If Player $-i$ always continues, $P_i(x) = -\infty$ $\rightarrow$ optimal to quit.

\item If Player $-i$ always quits, optimal to continue.

\end{itemize}


\textbf{Mixed strategies}: for one player to randomize, she needs to be indifferent between
quit and continue, which is only possible if the other player also randomizes. If Player $-i$
continues with probability $q$:


\begin{formula}

\[
P_i(x; C) = q(-0.1 + P_i(x)) + (1-q)(0.9 - x)
\]

\[
P_i(x; Q) = -x
\]

Player $i$ is indifferent if the two payouts are the same:

\[
q = 0.9
\]

\end{formula}


\begin{important}

\textbf{3 strategy profiles that are NE}:
\begin{itemize}

\item $(Quit, Continue)$

\item $(Continue, Quit)$

\item $((0.9C, 0.1Q), (0.9C, 0.1Q))$

\end{itemize}

Only one of these three, \textbf{(Continue, Quit)}, survives if the game has a finite
horizon -- even if the final time is after, say, 1 million periods, and no player would ever
want to get there! Problematic feature of backward induction\dots

\end{important}


\begin{example}

\textbf{In lab experiments}: people continue to bid without problem until the highest bid
reaches \$1. Afterwards, they start to feel uncomfortable but often continue to outbid each
other for a while. Typically the dollar ends up being sold for \$3 (plus almost \$3 paid by
the second bidder).

The concept of \textbf{sunk cost} is difficult to digest for non-experienced players.

\end{example}

\section{Games of Incomplete Information (Bayesian Games)}


So far, everything about the game was \textbf{common knowledge}: who are the players, what are
their preferences, what are the payouts. But players might have private information about
their own payout, their ``type'' or preferences, etc.


\begin{definition}

\textbf{BAYESIAN GAME} (simultaneous-move game of incomplete information)

Defined by:

\begin{itemize}

\item Players

\item Action set for each player

\item Possible types $\theta_i$ for each player $i$ and a probability distribution over
player $i$ types $p(\theta_i)$.

\end{itemize}

A pure strategy is a plan of action \textbf{for each type of player}.

\end{definition}


\begin{definition}

\textbf{BAYESIAN NASH EQUILIBRIUM (BNE)}

A strategy profile $(s_1(\theta_1), s_2(\theta_2), \dots, s_N(\theta_N))$ is a BNE of a
Bayesian game with $N$ players iff, for each player $i$, her \textbf{expected payout} using
strategy $i$ is higher than or equal to her expected payout using any of the other available
strategies, keeping fixed the strategies of the other players, which \textbf{depend on their
types}:

\[
E[\mathcal{P}_i(s_i(\theta_i), s_{-i}(\theta_{-i}) | \theta_i)] \ge
E[\mathcal{P}_i(\tilde{s}_i(\theta_i), s_{-i}(\theta_{-i}) | \theta_i)]
\]

The expected value is taken over all possible types of the other players.

\end{definition}

\section{Example: Public Good and Free Riding}


\begin{example}

\begin{itemize}

\item Two players can work (W) or not work (N) to produce a public good, that would give
utility 1 to both players.

\item If at least one player works, the public good is available for both. If nobody works,
it is not available.

\item Working has a cost $c_1$ for Player 1 and $c_2$ for Player 2.

\end{itemize}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{c|cc}
$1 \backslash 2$ & W & N \\
\hline
W & $(1-c_1, 1-c_2)$ & $(1-c_1, 1)$ \\
N & $(1, 1-c_2)$ & $(0,0)$ \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{itemize}

\item $c_1 = 0.5$, known to everybody.

\item $c_2$ is known to Player 2 but not to Player 1: it can be 0.2 with probability $p$
and 1.2 with probability $1-p$.

\item \textbf{The value of $p$ is common knowledge.}

\end{itemize}

\end{example}


Equivalently, Player 2 can be of two \textbf{types}: \textbf{Efficient} (E, cost of working
$c_2 = 0.2$, probability $p$) or \textbf{Inefficient} (I, $c_2 = 1.2$, probability $1-p$).


\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{c|cc}
\multicolumn{3}{c}{prob $p$} \\
$1 \backslash$ 2E & W & N \\
\hline
W & (0.5,0.8) & (0.5,1) \\
N & (1,0.8) & (0,0) \\
\end{tabular}
\quad
\begin{tabular}{c|cc}
\multicolumn{3}{c}{prob $(1-p)$} \\
$1 \backslash$ 2I & W & N \\
\hline
W & (0.5,-0.2) & (0.5,1) \\
N & (1,-0.2) & (0,0) \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}


\begin{important}

`Work' is a \textbf{dominated} strategy for the Inefficient type of Player 2.

\end{important}


\separator


\textbf{Scenario 1: Efficient Player 2 works, Inefficient Player 2 does not work.}
Player 1's best reaction?

\begin{formula}

\begin{itemize}

\item Player 1 works: $\mathcal{P}_1 = 0.5$ for sure.

\item Player 1 does not work: payout 1 if Player 2 works (E type), 0 otherwise (I type):

\[
\mathcal{P}_1 = p \times 1 + (1-p) \times 0 = p
\]

\end{itemize}

$\Rightarrow$ Player 1 works if $p < 0.5$, and does not work if $p > 0.5$ (indifferent if
$p = 0.5$).

\end{formula}


\begin{itemize}

\item For $p > 0.5$ Player 1 does not work (for $p = 0.5$ he may not work). Knowing this,
Player 2 works when he is of type E: the starting point was correct.

$\Rightarrow$ \textbf{If $p \ge 0.5$, one BNE is $(N, WN)$}: N for Player 1, W for Player 2
of type E, N for Player 2 of type I.

\item If $p < 0.5$, Player 1 works; but then Player 2 will not work even if he is of
efficient type\dots Hence for $p < 0.5$ this scenario is not a BNE.

\end{itemize}


\textbf{Scenario 2: Player 2 never works.} Player 1 gets $1 - c_1 = 0.5$ if he works and 0
if he does not $\Rightarrow$ regardless of $p$, Player 1 works; and when Player 1 works,
Player 2 prefers not to work (1 > 0.8 and 1 > -0.2). \textbf{BNE: $(W, NN)$}.


\begin{important}

\begin{itemize}

\item If $p < 0.5$: unique pure-strategy BNE \textbf{$(W, NN)$}.

\item If $p \ge 0.5$: two pure-strategy BNE \textbf{$(W, NN)$} and \textbf{$(N, WN)$}.

\end{itemize}

\end{important}
