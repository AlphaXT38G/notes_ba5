\section{Sequential Games}


\begin{important}

\begin{itemize}

\item Lectures 1-2: each player makes only one choice, and all choose simultaneously.

\item This week: players make (one or multiple) choices \textbf{sequentially}, as in chess.

\item Main message: the Nash equilibrium solution concept is \textbf{not fully satisfactory}
for sequential games. We refine it with the \textbf{Subgame-perfect equilibrium}.

\end{itemize}

\end{important}


\begin{definition}

\textbf{SEQUENTIAL GAME}

Needs to specify:

\begin{itemize}

\item the players;

\item the order of moves (which player moves at time 0, 1, 2\dots?);

\item the possible moves of each player at each of her decision points;

\item the information available to players throughout the game.

\end{itemize}

\end{definition}


\begin{definition}

\textbf{PERFECT INFORMATION}

At each time 0, 1, 2, \dots\ each player knows the actions taken by the other players up to
that time (assumed in this lecture).

\end{definition}



%=========================================================
\section{Example: Ultimatum Game}


\begin{example}

\begin{itemize}

\item Players: 1 and 2.

\item Player 1 has 3 coins, but needs to share them with Player 2.

\item First, Player 1 makes an offer to Player 2: he can offer 1, 2 or 3 coins.

\item Next, Player 2 can accept or reject the offer. If he accepts, he gets the $n$ (1, 2 or
3) coins offered and Player 1 keeps the rest. If Player 2 rejects, nobody gets anything.

\end{itemize}

\end{example}


\begin{definition}

\textbf{EXTENSIVE FORM}

Sequential games are best represented in extensive form (= as \textbf{decision trees}).

\end{definition}

\section{Pure Strategies and Normal Form}


\begin{definition}

\textbf{PURE STRATEGY (SEQUENTIAL GAME)}

A pure strategy for Player $i$ in a sequential game of perfect information is a
\textbf{contingent plan} that specifies an action at \textbf{each decision node} of
Player $i$.

\end{definition}


\begin{example}

Ultimatum game: a pure strategy for Player 1 could be simply ``Offer 1''. A pure strategy for
Player 2 could be ``Reject if the offer is 1 or 2, and accept if the offer is 3''.

\end{example}


\textbf{Normal-form representation of the Ultimatum game}

\begin{center}
\footnotesize
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{2pt}
\begin{tabular}{c|cccccccc}
$1 \backslash 2$ & RRR & RRA & RAA & AAA & ARR & RAR & AAR & ARA \\
\hline
Offer 1 & (0,0) & (0,0) & (0,0) & (2,1) & (2,1) & (0,0) & (2,1) & (2,1) \\
Offer 2 & (0,0) & (0,0) & (1,2) & (1,2) & (0,0) & (1,2) & (1,2) & (0,0) \\
Offer 3 & (0,0) & (0,3) & (0,3) & (0,3) & (0,0) & (0,0) & (0,0) & (0,3) \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}


The eight choices for Player 2 are his eight possible pure strategies: e.g.\ `RRR' means
Reject if offered 1, 2 or 3; `RAR' means Reject if offered 1, Accept if offered 2, Reject if
offered 3.

\section{Nash Equilibrium of Sequential Games}


\begin{definition}

\textbf{NASH EQUILIBRIUM}

Defined the same way as for simultaneous-move games: a strategy profile
$(s^{(1)}, s^{(2)}, \dots, s^{(N)})$ is a Nash equilibrium iff

\[
\mathcal{P}^{(i)}(s^{(i)}, s^{(-i)}) \ge \mathcal{P}^{(i)}(\tilde{s}^{(i)}, s^{(-i)})
\quad \text{for every possible } \tilde{s}^{(i)}
\]

i.e.\ the best that one player can do \emph{given the strategies of all the other players}.

\end{definition}


\begin{example}

\textbf{The Ultimatum game has many pure-strategy NE} (underlined):

\begin{center}
\footnotesize
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{2pt}
\begin{tabular}{c|cccccccc}
$1 \backslash 2$ & RRR & RRA & RAA & AAA & ARR & RAR & AAR & ARA \\
\hline
Offer 1 & (0,0) & (0,0) & (0,0) & \underline{(2,1)} & \underline{(2,1)} & (0,0) & \underline{(2,1)} & \underline{(2,1)} \\
Offer 2 & (0,0) & (0,0) & \underline{(1,2)} & (1,2) & (0,0) & \underline{(1,2)} & (1,2) & (0,0) \\
Offer 3 & (0,0) & \underline{(0,3)} & (0,3) & (0,3) & (0,0) & (0,0) & (0,0) & (0,3) \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Take for example the NE (Offer 2, RAA): Player 2 will not accept unless he is offered at
least 2. Player 1 knows it, and therefore offers 2.

\end{example}



%=========================================================
\section{Non-credible Threats}


\begin{warning}

The NE (Offer 2, RAA) involves a \textbf{non-credible threat} by Player 2: that he will
Reject (and therefore get nothing) if he is offered 1.

But once Player 1 offers 1, it would be irrational for Player 2 to reject the offer and walk
away with nothing!

\end{warning}

\section{The Entry-Deterrence Game}


\begin{definition}

\textbf{STRATEGIC ENTRY DETERRENCE}

In business, any action taken by an existing business in a particular market that discourages
potential entrants from entering into competition in that market (barriers to entry: hostile
takeovers, heavy spending on new product development, predatory pricing\dots).

\end{definition}

Enter & \underline{(1,-1)} & (-3,-3) \\

Two pure-strategy NE: \textbf{(Not Enter, Fight)} and \textbf{(Enter, Accept)}.

But (Not Enter, Fight) is again a \textbf{non-credible threat}.

\end{important}



%=========================================================
\section{Sequential Rationality}


\begin{definition}

\textbf{SEQUENTIAL RATIONALITY}

A player's equilibrium strategy should specify \textbf{optimal actions at every decision
node} in a tree.

\end{definition}


\begin{example}

\begin{itemize}

\item \textbf{Entry deterrence}: Fight is a suboptimal decision in the decision node that
starts after New Firm chooses `Enter'. Therefore, the NE (Not Enter, Fight) is not
realistic.

\item \textbf{Ultimatum game}: the NE (Offer 2, RAA) -- and also (Offer 2, RAR) and
(Offer 3, RRA) -- do not satisfy the Sequential Rationality principle either.

\end{itemize}

\end{example}


\begin{important}

We need a refinement of the concept of Nash equilibrium for a sequential game, that satisfies
sequential rationality.

\end{important}

\section{Subgames}


\begin{definition}

\textbf{SUBGAME}

Given a game $G$, a subgame $G_1$ is the portion of the game $G$ starting at one of the
decision nodes and containing all its successor nodes.

\end{definition}


\begin{example}

Subgames of the Ultimatum game: the whole game (yellow) and the three subgames starting at
the decision nodes of Player 2 (green).

\section{Subgame-Perfect Equilibrium (SPE)}


\begin{definition}

\textbf{SUBGAME-PERFECT EQUILIBRIUM}

A subgame-perfect equilibrium of an extensive-form game $G$ is a strategy profile $s$ such
that for any subgame $G'$, the restriction of $s$ to $G'$ is a Nash equilibrium for $G'$.

\end{definition}


\begin{important}

A subgame-perfect equilibrium is a special kind of Nash equilibrium (obvious since $G$ is a
subgame of itself).

\end{important}



%=========================================================
\section{Backward Induction}


\begin{summary}

\textbf{Backward induction} -- the technique to obtain a Subgame-Perfect equilibrium:

\begin{enumerate}

\item Find the Nash equilibria of the smallest subgames.

\item Replace each subgame with the payoff of (one of) its NE.

\item Repeat the procedure with the reduced game.

\end{enumerate}

\end{summary}


\begin{example}

\textbf{Entry-Deterrence game}

\begin{itemize}

\item The smallest subgame (after `Enter') has only one player, Old Firm, who will choose
`Accept'. We replace this subgame with the payout of (Enter, Accept): $(1,-1)$.

\item The reduced game (Not Enter: $(0,0)$, (Enter, A): $(1,-1)$) has only itself as a
subgame. It is a one-player game (New Firm), who will choose Enter.

\end{itemize}

SPE: \textbf{(Enter, Accept)}.

\end{example}


\begin{example}

\textbf{Ultimatum game}

\begin{itemize}

\item In each of the smallest subgames there is only Player 2, and in each of these the only
NE is `Accept'.

\item After replacing them with the payout of their NE, we obtain the reduced game:

\end{itemize}

\begin{center}

\includegraphics[
width=0.75\linewidth
]{../images/week03_04.png}

\end{center}

\begin{itemize}

\item Only Player 1 needs to choose, and will choose `Offer 1'.

\end{itemize}

SPE: \textbf{(Offer 1, AAA)}.

\end{example}

\section{An Example with two SPE}


\begin{example}

New version of the Ultimatum Game: Player 1 still has 3 coins, but can now also offer 0 coins
to Player 2 (so can offer 0, 1, 2, or 3 coins).

\begin{center}

\includegraphics[
width=0.95\linewidth
]{../images/week03_05.png}

\end{center}

\end{example}


\begin{itemize}

\item In the subgame starting after `Offer 0', Player 2 gets 0 whether he accepts or rejects:
`Accept' and `Reject' are \textbf{two pure-strategy NE} of this subgame.

\item In the other three subgames, the only NE is `Accept'.

\end{itemize}


\begin{important}

\textbf{Solution 1}: choose the NE `Accept' for all 4 subgames. Reduced game: (Offer 0,A)
$(3,0)$, (Offer 1,A) $(2,1)$, (Offer 2,A) $(1,2)$, (Offer 3,A) $(0,3)$. Player 1 chooses
`Offer 0', which gives her the highest payout (3).

SPE: \textbf{(Offer 0, AAAA)}.

\separator

\textbf{Solution 2}: choose the NE `Reject' for the subgame starting after `Offer 0', and the
(only) NE `Accept' for the other 3 subgames. Reduced game: (Offer 0,R) $(0,0)$, (Offer 1,A)
$(2,1)$, (Offer 2,A) $(1,2)$, (Offer 3,A) $(0,3)$. Player 1 chooses `Offer 1', which gives
her the highest payout (2).

SPE: \textbf{(Offer 1, RAAA)}.

\end{important}

\section{Threat Credibility: Cuban Missile Crisis (1962)}


Threat credibility is very important in politics and international relations. Game theory
tools have been used to analyze the \textbf{Cuban missile crisis in 1962}: one of the most
dramatic points in the Cold War, unfolding over 13 days, in which many feared that a nuclear
war might be approaching.


\begin{example}

\begin{itemize}

\item The USA, led by President JFK, discovered that \textbf{the USSR was in the process of
installing missiles in Cuba}, to deter US attacks to the island and more generally to provoke
and threaten the USA.

\item The main ``punishments'' the USA discussed were a \textbf{naval blockade to Cuba} and
an \textbf{air-strike to destroy the missiles}.

\item The USSR, hoping that the USA would just accept the missiles without retaliation, made
it known that both these options would be acts of war, that would \textbf{inevitably lead to
a nuclear war between USA and USSR}.

\end{itemize}

\end{example}


\textbf{Order of preference over final outcomes}

\textbf{USSR}:

\begin{enumerate}

\item Missiles installed, USA accepts without retaliation

\item Status Quo (No Missiles installed)

\item Missiles installed, USA retaliation through naval blockade, no war

\item Missiles installed, USA retaliation through air strike, no war

\item Nuclear War

\end{enumerate}

\textbf{USA}:

\begin{enumerate}

\item Status Quo (No Missiles installed)

\item Missiles installed, USA retaliation through naval blockade, no war

\item Missiles installed, USA retaliation through air strike, no war

\item Missiles installed, USA accepts without retaliation

\item Nuclear War

\end{enumerate}


\begin{center}

\includegraphics[
width=0.8\linewidth
]{../images/week03_06.png}

\end{center}


\begin{warning}

Different payouts preserving the same preference ranking for USA and USSR would lead to the
same NE and SPE.

\end{warning}


\textbf{Normal form and NE} (underlined). USSR strategies: first letter = first move (N:
Do Nothing, M: Missiles), then the answer (W: War, A: Accept) after an Air Strike, then after
a Blockade.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{c|ccc}
USSR $\backslash$ USA & Air Strike & Blockade & Accept \\
\hline
NWW & \underline{(4,5)} & \underline{(4,5)} & (4,5) \\
NWA & \underline{(4,5)} & \underline{(4,5)} & (4,5) \\
NAW & \underline{(4,5)} & \underline{(4,5)} & (4,5) \\
NAA & \underline{(4,5)} & \underline{(4,5)} & (4,5) \\
MWW & (1,1) & (1,1) & \underline{(5,2)} \\
MWA & (1,1) & (3,4) & (5,2) \\
MAW & (2,3) & (1,1) & (5,2) \\
MAA & (2,3) & (3,4) & (5,2) \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}


\begin{important}

Of all the NE, only \textbf{(NAA, Blockade)} is a SPE.

The USSR was counting on the USA to believe that the MWW strategy would be credible, so that
the realized NE would be (MWW, Accept). Eventually, the USSR agreed to remove the missiles,
consistently with the (NAA, Blockade) SPE.

\end{important}

\section{Weekly Summary}


\begin{summary}

\begin{itemize}

\item Sequential games are represented in extensive form (decision trees).

\item A pure strategy is a contingent plan: an action at each decision node of the player.

\item The NE of sequential games can rely on \textbf{non-credible threats}.

\item \textbf{Sequential rationality}: optimal actions at every decision node.

\item \textbf{SPE}: the restriction to any subgame is a NE of that subgame.

\item SPE are found by \textbf{backward induction}; a game can have several SPE.

\item If we have time: the ``pirate game'' video, a 5-person game solved by backward
induction (see week 04).

\end{itemize}

\end{summary}
