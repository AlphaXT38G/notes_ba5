how technical decisions in software have ethical consequences, and practical strategies to
anticipate and reduce them. They follow the chapters of the course (Introduction, Safety 1 \& 2,
Fairness 1 \& 2): the theory of the videos, the strategies (with their cheat sheets), and the
review questions and case studies of the Monday sessions.

simplest possible words. Each chapter ends with a big purple box, the \emph{Exam-ready recap}:
read only these boxes and you have the essentials.

\section{AI incidents and ethical debt}

\begin{exbox}[title={The OECD AI Incidents and Hazards Monitor (AIM)}]
A tool (\href{https://oecd.ai/en/incidents}{oecd.ai/en/incidents}) that collects AI-related
incidents and hazards reported in the press.
\begin{itemize}
  \item The \textbf{raw number} of AI incidents and hazards is \textbf{increasing}.
  \item Their \textbf{proportion} among all AI-related events reported is relatively stable
        (slightly decreasing): AI events in general and AI incidents both increase.
  \item An \emph{incident} is harm that happened; a \emph{hazard} is an event that could lead
        to harm.
  \item Limitations: it only sees what the press reports (bias towards some countries,
        languages and spectacular cases).
\end{itemize}
\end{exbox}

\begin{defnbox}[title={Ethical debt (Petrozzino, 2021)}]
The \textbf{price paid by people and the environment} because of systems that are
\textbf{released with ethical flaws}. Like technical debt, it accumulates when issues are
ignored to go faster, but it is paid by others.
\end{defnbox}

\begin{propbox}[title={Where does ethical debt come from?}]
AI and software engineers \emph{are} concerned by ethical questions, but:
\begin{itemize}
  \item competition and time pressure; organisational incentives;
  \item inherited practices and culture; who has the decision-making power;
  \item beliefs about tool neutrality (``it's just a tool'');
  \item lack of ethical knowledge;
  \item not seeing the ethical implications of technical decisions: a \textbf{``veiled''
        ethical agency} (Herkert et al., 2020; Widder et al., 2023).
\end{itemize}
\textbf{This is what the course is about.}
\end{propbox}

\begin{goatbox}
When people ship software with ethical problems to go fast, someone else pays later: that is
ethical debt. It happens because engineers are rushed and often do not even \emph{see} that a
technical choice is also an ethical choice. The course teaches you to see it.
\end{goatbox}

  \item In open questions: \textbf{1 point $\approx$ 1 to 2 complete sentences}; show your logic,

\end{itemize}

\chapter{Introduction: responsible software and ethics}

\begin{rembox}[title={In a nutshell}]
\textbf{What is this chapter about?} What ``responsible software'' means, what ethics is, how

\textbf{Why is it useful?} Every later chapter uses this vocabulary (issue, dilemma, ethical

\end{rembox}


\section{Responsible software}

\begin{defnbox}[title={Responsible software (van de Poel, 2011)}]
The focus is on \textbf{human responsibility}, and on \textbf{active}, forward-looking
responsibility (preventing harm, not only finding who is to blame afterwards). \\
Responsible software = software that is \textbf{engineered in a responsible way}: it is about
the \textbf{engineering process}, not a property of the code.
\end{defnbox}

\begin{defnbox}[title={Responsible engineering}]
Making engineering design \textbf{decisions} responsibly:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item with the goal to \textbf{do good};
  \item \textbf{preventing avoidable negative impacts};
  \item \textbf{taking into account} people, other systems, social structures and the planet.
\end{enumerate}
``The way a technology is designed determines its possibilities \ldots\ and has consequences
for human wellbeing'' (Roeser, 2012). The ACM code (2018) asks professionals to reflect upon the
wider impacts of their work, supporting the public good.
\end{defnbox}

\begin{defnbox}[title={Ethics}]
The \textbf{principles and standards that guide human behaviour} in various contexts. Three
branches:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Metaethics}: what does it mean for an action to be right?
  \item \textbf{Normative ethics}: how should people act in general (moral principles)?
  \item \textbf{Applied ethics}: how can I act ethically in a real-world situation?
        $\leftarrow$ \textbf{this course}.
\end{itemize}
\end{defnbox}

\begin{propbox}[title={How engineering organises responsibility}]
\begin{itemize}
  \item Norms and standards (ISO, SIA); obligations (qualifications, certifications); codes of
        conduct / ethics: they aim at preventing negative effects of systems on people and the
        environment.
  \item Plus \textbf{responsible design methodologies} (``not magic!''): the strategies of
        this course.
\end{itemize}
Examples of negative effects: software of autonomous cars and planes; social media and body
image / eating disorders (Dane \& Bhatia, 2023). How can we anticipate such consequences?
\end{propbox}

\begin{keybox}[title={Ethics goes beyond the law}]
An autonomous car's braking system meets all safety regulations but can fail in extreme
weather. An engineer may still argue for a recall: \textbf{ethics guides behaviour towards
doing the right thing, beyond compliance with the law}.
\end{keybox}

\begin{goatbox}
Responsible software is not ``nice code'': it is a way of making decisions while you build
software, so that it helps people and does not hurt them, even when the law would let you do
otherwise. Ethics is the set of rules of good behaviour; here we use it in practice.
\end{goatbox}


\section{Ethics in technical decisions}

\begin{exbox}[title={A ``simple'' form}]
A customer form allows one first name and one last name, no apostrophes, and names that can
never change. Purely technical choices, but they \textbf{exclude people} because of their names
(``falsehoods programmers believe about names'', Rogers, 2018).
\end{exbox}

\begin{defnbox}[title={Ethical issue}]
Any situation that may compromise, in whole or in part, the respect of at least one
\textbf{ethical value or principle}. Technical decisions can have ethical implications.
\end{defnbox}

\begin{defnbox}[title={Ethical agency}]
The capacity to make decisions and take actions \textbf{aligned with ethical values and
principles}.
\end{defnbox}

\begin{propbox}[title={Ethical decision-making (Rest, 1986; Schwartz, 2016)}]
\begin{center}
\textbf{Recognize} $\;\longrightarrow\;$ \textbf{Evaluate} $\;\longrightarrow\;$ \textbf{Act}
\end{center}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Ethical sensitivity} (to recognize): the ability to recognize ethical issues
        (\emph{awareness}) and to identify their impact (\emph{who} is affected, \emph{what}
        happens).
  \item It grows with \textbf{knowledge}, \textbf{experience} and \textbf{habits}, which is
        exactly the format of the course.
\end{itemize}
\end{propbox}

\begin{propbox}[title={Ethical lenses (IDEO, 2015; Isaac, 2023)}]
A product is usually judged on \textbf{desirability} (customer), \textbf{viability} (business),
\textbf{feasibility} (technology). Add a fourth lens: \textbf{ethicality} (people and
environment). The course chapters: 0 Introduction, 1--2 Safety, 3--4 Fairness, 5--6
Sustainability, 7--8 Empowerment, 9 Conclusion.
\end{propbox}

\begin{goatbox}
Even a choice as boring as ``how many letters in a name field'' can be unfair to someone. Being
ethical starts with \emph{noticing} that: see the problem, think about it, then act.
\end{goatbox}


\section{Ethical dilemmas}

\begin{defnbox}[title={Ethical dilemma (Swisher, 2005; van de Poel \& Royakkers, 2011)}]
A decision between two or more alternatives which \textbf{all conflict with ethical values or
principles}, none of which overrides the others. A genuine dilemma is a
\textbf{wrong--wrong choice}. Example: the trolley problem (do nothing: wrong; pull the lever:
also wrong); the Moral Machine experiment (Awad et al., 2018).
\end{defnbox}

\begin{warnbox}[title={Technical issue, ethical issue or dilemma?}]
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Technical issue}: a problem of the system (performance, bug) with no value at
        stake.
  \item \textbf{Ethical issue}: one value may be compromised.
  \item \textbf{Ethical dilemma}: whatever you choose, some value is compromised (so a
        dilemma contains at least two ethical issues).
\end{itemize}
\end{warnbox}

\begin{exbox}[title={Review questions of the session}]
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Brain-to-speech software must capture intentional communication only, not
        private inner thoughts}: an \textbf{ethical issue} (privacy). A dilemma can be seen
        behind it: help people with speech impairment (inclusion) vs.\ not capturing their
        thoughts (privacy).
  \item \emph{An ML model that discovers medicines can also discover chemical weapons}: an
        \textbf{ethical dilemma} (dual use).
  \item \emph{Students' chatbot conversations sent to teachers to catch hallucinations}: an
        \textbf{ethical dilemma}: students may learn wrong things (safety) vs.\ their
        conversations are read by the teacher (privacy, imbalance of power). And the
        ``solution'' does not scale.
\end{itemize}
\end{exbox}

\begin{defnbox}[title={Dual use (Imperiale \& Casadevall, 2015; Forge, 2010)}]
Technologies that can be applied for both \textbf{beneficial and harmful} purposes (the harmful
one possibly by others). Key dilemma: develop it for its benefits but risk harm, or do not
develop it and forgo the benefits. Not specific to software, but software is increasingly
concerned: \textbf{generative AI is a dual-use technology} (Ferrara, 2024). (``Dual use'' also
means civilian + military, e.g.\ the EU Dual Use Regulation.)
\end{defnbox}

\begin{propbox}[title={Exit, Voice, Loyalty + Insubordination: ``EVIL'' (Lennerfors, 2021; Hirschman, 1972)}]
A heuristic when facing a dilemma, in particular wrongdoing in your organisation:
\textbf{Exit} (quit the project), \textbf{Voice} (raise your concerns to other actors),
\textbf{Loyalty} (do what is asked), \textbf{Insubordination} (e.g.\ secretly not doing it, or
sabotage). Each option has its own dilemma and depends on the context (can you afford to lose
your job?).
\end{propbox}

\begin{defnbox}[title={Judgment (the Evaluate step)}]
Evaluating the options to determine the most ethically appropriate one. It is both
\textbf{cognitive} (reason, rationalization) and \textbf{affective} (emotion, intuition).
\end{defnbox}

\begin{defnbox}[title={Moral emotions (Roeser, 2012; Tangney et al., 2007)}]
Emotions experienced in response to moral issues, or that motivate (im)moral behaviour. They
can be used explicitly in responsible design, as \textbf{indicators of risks}.
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{l|l|l}
 & \textbf{Negative} & \textbf{Positive} \\ \hline
\textbf{Self}  & guilt, shame, embarrassment & pride, self-forgiveness \\
\textbf{Other} & anger, contempt, disgust    & gratitude, elevation, forgiveness
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\end{defnbox}

\begin{defnbox}[title={Normative ethical theories}]
\begin{center}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{l l X}
\textbf{Theory} & \textbf{Focus} & \textbf{Right action} \\ \hline
Utilitarianism & consequences  & the greatest good for the greatest number \\
Deontology     & the action    & do the right thing according to universal principles (duties, rules) \\
Virtue ethics  & the person    & act as a virtuous person would (honest, courageous, \ldots) \\
Care ethics    & relationships & mutual responsibility and care for each other
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
\end{defnbox}

\begin{exbox}[title={Exam-type questions}]
\begin{itemize}
  \item ``It is always wrong to lie, even if telling the truth might harm some people'' $\Rightarrow$
        \textbf{deontology} (a universal principle).
  \item ``If I do not report this bug, I am not being a trustworthy and courageous person''
        $\Rightarrow$ \textbf{virtue} ethics.
\end{itemize}
\end{exbox}

\begin{algbox}[title={An ethical decision-making framework (Markkula Center, 2021)}]
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Identify the ethical issue(s).
  \item Get the facts.
  \item Evaluate alternative actions with \textbf{several} ethical theories.
  \item Choose an option and ``test'' it.
  \item Implement it and reflect on the outcome.
\end{enumerate}
Ethical problems are ill-defined: there is no single correct answer, but there are better
argued ones.
\end{algbox}

\begin{goatbox}
A dilemma is when every door leads to some harm: you cannot be perfectly good, you can only
pick the least bad door and explain why. To pick, look at consequences (utilitarianism), rules
(deontology), what a good person would do (virtue) and relationships (care). Your gut feelings
(guilt, anger\ldots) are useful alarms too.
\end{goatbox}


\section{Strategy: stakeholder analysis}

\begin{defnbox}[title={Stakeholder (Value Sensitive Design)}]
``Individuals, groups, organizations, institutions, and societies that might reasonably be
affected by the technology.'' Any entity on which the software can have an impact,
\textbf{positive or negative}, \textbf{direct or indirect}. A stakeholder can be
\textbf{non-human} (e.g.\ the environment). Example: ChatGPT also affects the hidden data
labellers hired to annotate its training data.
\end{defnbox}

\begin{algbox}[title={Applying the strategy (cheat sheet 0.4)}]
\textbf{Goal}: identify the stakeholders of a product as exhaustively as possible.
\textbf{When}: early in the life cycle, so that nobody is forgotten.
\textbf{Question}: \emph{who is impacted by my software?}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Brainstorm} a range of stakeholders with questions:
        who uses the product daily? who is geographically close to it or its users? socially
        close to the users? whose activities are created, modified or replaced? who maintains
        it? who provides the services it needs to run? who funds it? which communities are
        active in the domain? (Markkula: who is it intended for, does it replace jobs, does a
        social / economic / racial / age / gender / religious group benefit or suffer more,
        legal risks, competitors\ldots)
  \item Use \textbf{direct vs.\ indirect} to categorize them and to find more, especially
        indirect ones.
\end{enumerate}
\end{algbox}

\begin{defnbox}[title={Direct vs.\ indirect stakeholders}]
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Direct}: \textbf{in contact with the software} (end-users, administrators,
        developers, contractors).
  \item \textbf{Indirect}: \textbf{impacted by its existence although they do not interact with
        it} (environment, government, neighbourhood, friends, NGOs, the public). Finding them
        anticipates non-obvious adverse impacts.
\end{itemize}
The boundary is not always clear-cut. Tesla Autopilot: car owners and developers (direct);
police, traffic authorities, other car companies, taxi drivers (indirect).
\end{defnbox}

\begin{keybox}[title={How the case studies are graded}]
A good justification makes clear \textbf{how} the stakeholder uses / interacts with the software
(direct) or \textbf{how} it is impacted without using it (indirect), and is \textbf{specific to
the scenario}. Weak: ``Healthcare professionals are end-users of the app''. Good: ``Paper
supplier (indirect): the software replaces paper-based records, so orders from the hospital
drop sharply: it is affected although it never uses the software.'' Tips: group similar
stakeholders; when unsure, state your hypothesis or argue both ways. In practice, interviews,
experts and mapping the social and technical systems help.
\end{keybox}

\begin{rembox}[title={Other approaches}]
Ethix (add \emph{unintended} stakeholders); Mitchell et al.\ (1997): salience from
\textbf{power, legitimacy, urgency}; Chevalier \& Buckles (SAS$^2$): chart stakeholders by their
impact from and influence on the product.
\end{rembox}

\begin{goatbox}
Before building, list everyone (and everything) your software touches: those who use it
(direct) and those who never touch it but still feel its effects (indirect), like the paper
seller who loses a client when a hospital goes digital.
\end{goatbox}


\section{Strategy: ethical speculation (``Escape the mirror'')}

\begin{defnbox}[title={Ethical speculation (Klassen \& Fiesler, 2022)}]
Anticipating all outcomes is impossible, so we \textbf{imagine} future scenarios
(``how to think, not what to think'', Burton et al., 2018), like the writers of the TV series
\emph{Black Mirror}. It trains creativity and helps to envision and prevent issues.
\textbf{When}: early in design, before major updates or new features.
\end{defnbox}

\begin{algbox}[title={Applying the strategy (cheat sheet 0.5)}]
\textbf{Part I --- dark and pessimistic} (a Black Mirror episode):
imagine a character negatively impacted by the software, write the \textbf{pitch} of their
story, find an attractive \textbf{title} (no image needed at the exam). \\
\textbf{Part II --- positive outcome}: identify 1 or 2 main \textbf{ethical issues} of the story,
describe the \textbf{immediate and future consequences}, imagine a \textbf{happy ending}.
\end{algbox}

\begin{warnbox}[title={Happy ending: not ``there should be a law''}]
It is too easy to say ``we should have laws for that''. Say what the people \textbf{creating}
the software can do to take responsibility and prevent the consequences, and how to reach the
good futures.
\end{warnbox}

\begin{exbox}[title={``Social Services 5.0''}]
Issue: bias in an AI allocating social services. Immediate: reduced access to essential
services for some people. Future: institutionalised bias, loss of trust in institutions. Happy
ending: the bias is revealed, fairness and transparency are required.
\end{exbox}

\begin{propbox}[title={The four ethical lenses of the course}]
\textbf{Safety} (the ways software can cause harm), \textbf{Fairness} (how different groups of
people can be treated differently by software), \textbf{Sustainability} (impact on the planet),
\textbf{Empowerment} (how software gives or removes control to people). Use them to classify the
issues of your story (e.g.\ ``some socio-economic groups have more misdiagnoses'' = fairness;
``people lose control over essential aspects of their lives'' = empowerment).
\end{propbox}

\begin{goatbox}
Write a scary episode where your app ruins someone's life, then rewrite the ending where the
makers of the app fix the problem. Imagining the disaster first is a cheap way to avoid it.
\end{goatbox}


\begin{recapbox}[title={Exam-ready recap --- Introduction}]
\textbf{Words to master}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Responsible software} = engineered responsibly: a \emph{process} of decisions
        that aim to do good, prevent avoidable harm, take people / systems / society / planet
        into account. Human, \emph{active} (forward-looking) responsibility.
  \item \textbf{Ethics} = principles guiding behaviour. Meta / normative / \textbf{applied}
        (= this course). Ethics goes \textbf{beyond the law}.
  \item \textbf{Ethical issue} = a value or principle may be compromised. \textbf{Ethical
        agency} = capacity to act according to values.
  \item \textbf{Ethical dilemma} = every option compromises a value (\textbf{wrong--wrong}).
        \textbf{Dual use} = beneficial and harmful uses (GenAI, drug discovery $\to$ weapons).
  \item Decision steps: \textbf{Recognize $\to$ Evaluate $\to$ Act}; ethical sensitivity =
        awareness + who/what is impacted.
  \item Normative theories: \textbf{utilitarianism} (consequences, greatest good),
        \textbf{deontology} (universal principles, duty), \textbf{virtue} (be a good person),
        \textbf{care} (relationships).
  \item Moral emotions are \textbf{risk indicators}. EVIL = Exit, Voice, Loyalty,
        Insubordination.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Strategies}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Stakeholder analysis}: brainstorm (daily users, close geographically /
        socially, activities replaced, maintenance, services, funding, communities), then
        \textbf{direct} (interacts with it) vs.\ \textbf{indirect} (affected without using it).
        Justify \emph{how}, specifically to the scenario.
  \item \textbf{Ethical speculation}: Part I dark story (character, pitch, title), Part II
        issues + immediate/future consequences + happy ending driven by the designers, not just
        ``a law''.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Typical traps}: an issue (one value) vs.\ a dilemma (two values in conflict); ``direct''
means \emph{interacts with the software}, not ``strongly affected''.
\end{recapbox}

\chapter{Safety 1: harms, threats and content moderation}
% =========================================================

\begin{rembox}[title={In a nutshell}]
\textbf{What is this chapter about?} The difference between safety and security, the four
scenarios in which software causes harm (malfunction, abuse, misuse, intended use), the harms of
user-generated content and the dilemma with free speech, how content moderation works, the
confusion matrix, and three strategies: \textbf{bad actors}, \textbf{STRIDE} and \textbf{harm
modeling}. \\
\textbf{Why is it useful?} These are the basic tools to anticipate \emph{how} software can hurt
people; the confusion matrix comes back in Fairness 2.
\end{rembox}


\section{Safety and security}

\begin{defnbox}[title={Security vs.\ safety (Leveson, 1986)}]
\begin{center}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{X|X}
\textbf{Security} & \textbf{Safety} \\ \hline
Preventing the \textbf{environment} from affecting negatively the \textbf{system} &
Preventing the \textbf{system} from affecting negatively the \textbf{environment} \\
\textbf{Threats}: \textbf{intentional} & \textbf{Hazards}: \textbf{accidental} \\
e.g.\ an e-shop attacked by hackers & e.g.\ X-ray analysis software that misses a disease \\
Methods: vulnerability scanning, ethical hacking, security audits, code review &
Methods: verification and testing, failure modes analysis, redundancy
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
Both are about \textbf{risks} = the probability that something bad happens. Both are needed:
an attack on an e-shop (security) can harm its customers (safety).
\end{defnbox}

\begin{exbox}[title={Exam-type questions: autonomous car}]
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{The car's software misreads 10\% of traffic signs}: a \textbf{safety hazard}
        (accidental, the system harms its environment).
  \item \emph{Stickers on a stop sign make the software read it as a speed limit}: a
        \textbf{security threat} (the environment, possibly bad actors, attacks the system)
        \textbf{and} a \textbf{safety hazard} (the car then endangers passengers and other
        road users).
\end{itemize}
\end{exbox}

\begin{defnbox}[title={Socio-technical system (Oosthuizen \& Pretorius, 2016)}]
A system where the \textbf{social system} (people, organisations, rules) and the
\textbf{technical system} affect each other: the internet, social media, ride sharing,
autonomous vehicles. Their behaviour is hard to anticipate (Sommerville, 2016): hidden loops,
emergence, non-determinism; it requires interdisciplinary approaches.
\end{defnbox}

\begin{keybox}
In this course, \textbf{safety} = limiting as much as possible the negative impacts software can
cause, seeing software as a socio-technical system.
\end{keybox}

\begin{goatbox}
Security: protect the fence from the wolf (someone attacks on purpose). Safety: make sure the
fence does not fall on the goats (harm by accident). A wolf breaking the fence can make it fall
on the goats: both at once.
\end{goatbox}


\section{When can software cause harm? Four scenarios}

\begin{defnbox}[title={Four scenarios of harm (Parasuraman \& Riley, 1997)}]
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Malfunction}: the software does not function or perform as specified, because
        of an \textbf{unintentional} defect, fault or bug. Severe in life-critical and
        mission-critical systems. Examples: Therac-25 radiotherapy overdoses (1985--87, Leveson
        \& Turner, 1993); the CrowdStrike outage (2024, blue screens worldwide).
  \item \textbf{Abuse}: attack, diversion or use of the software for \textbf{malicious}
        purposes, by authorized or unauthorized users (bad actors / threat actors), with direct
        and indirect impacts. Examples: ransomware on hospitals; encrypted messaging used by
        organised crime.
  \item \textbf{Misuse}: use in a way \textbf{not intended or not (officially) supported},
        accidental or careless: lack of understanding, mismatch between design and
        expectations, over-reliance, insufficient monitoring. Examples: Samsung employees
        leaking proprietary code into ChatGPT (2023); a Deloitte report to the Australian
        government with made-up quotes (2025).
  \item \textbf{Intended use}: the software is used \textbf{correctly, as planned}, and still
        causes harm. The hardest to anticipate: it emerges from human + system behaviour, scale
        and time. Examples: infinite scrolling and loss of self-control (Park \& Jung, 2024);
        social media influence on elections (Aral \& Eckles, 2019).
\end{itemize}
The line between misuse and abuse is not always clear: the question is \textbf{intent}.
\end{defnbox}

\begin{exbox}[title={Review question}]
Ecosystems damaged by viral travel trends on social media: \textbf{intended use} (people share
and follow posts exactly as the platform intends).
\end{exbox}

\begin{goatbox}
Four ways your tool hurts someone: it breaks (malfunction), a bad guy uses it to hurt
(abuse), someone uses it wrongly without meaning harm (misuse), or it works perfectly and
still hurts (intended use, the sneakiest).
\end{goatbox}


\section{Safety and user-generated content}

\begin{defnbox}[title={User-generated content, UGC (Bolin, 2021; Naab \& Sehl, 2016)}]
Any form of content (images, videos, texts) \textbf{posted by users} on online platforms (social
media, forums, wikis), published publicly, as opposed to professional or institutional content.
Web 2.0 (TIME person of the year 2006: ``You''); Wikipedia: about 7M articles, 46M users.
\end{defnbox}

\begin{propbox}[title={How UGC creates harm (Banko et al., 2020)}]
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Hate and harassment} (threats of violence, identity misrepresentation);
  \item \textbf{Self-inflicted harm} (promotion of eating disorders);
  \item \textbf{Ideological harm} (extremism, terrorism, misinformation);
  \item \textbf{Exploitation} (sexual abuse, scams).
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Toxic content} = material that is harmful or offensive, with a negative impact on
individuals and communities. Types of harm (Scheuerman et al., 2021): physical, emotional,
relational, financial.
\end{propbox}

\begin{defnbox}[title={Hate speech (United Nations, 2019)}]
Three attributes: (1) \textbf{discriminatory or pejorative} of an individual or group; (2)
calls out real or perceived \textbf{identity factors} (religion, ethnicity, nationality, race,
gender\ldots); (3) conveyed through \textbf{any form of expression} (images, memes, symbols).
There is no commonly agreed legal definition. On UGC platforms it is easily produced and shared
(often anonymously), reaches a global audience in real time, and recommendation algorithms may
unintentionally amplify it.
\end{defnbox}

\begin{exbox}[title={Facebook and the Rohingya (Myanmar, 2017)}]
Hate speech and military disinformation spread on Facebook before the genocide; moderation tools
were lacking and the reaction slow. The UN Human Rights Council (2018): Facebook ``has been a
useful instrument for those seeking to spread hate''.
\end{exbox}

\begin{defnbox}[title={Freedom of expression (UDHR art.\ 19, 1948; ICCPR, 1966)}]
``Everyone has the right to freedom of opinion and expression; this right includes freedom to
hold opinions without interference and to seek, receive and impart information and ideas through
any media and regardless of frontiers.'' Independent of the format; \textbf{not absolute}
(must respect other rights, no incitement to discrimination or violence); a pillar of democracy.
UGC supports it: exchange of ideas, civic debate and dissent, transparency on conflicts and
corruption (Segado-Boj, 2020).
\end{defnbox}

\begin{keybox}[title={The dilemma: free speech vs.\ toxic content}]
Filtering toxic content makes two kinds of errors:
\textbf{false negatives} (toxic content that escapes the filter: harm to people) and
\textbf{false positives} (harmless content wrongly censored: harm to free speech). Lowering one
raises the other. \textbf{Who decides?} States (protect human rights in their jurisdiction,
laws differ), platforms (private, global, with business interests), people (responsible for
what they publish). What is legal is not always what is ethical: a multi-stakeholder question.
\end{keybox}

\begin{goatbox}
People post things online. Some posts hurt others (hate, scams). If you delete too little,
people get hurt; if you delete too much, you silence people who did nothing wrong. There is no
perfect setting, so someone has to choose, and that choice is ethical.
\end{goatbox}


\section{Content moderation}

\begin{defnbox}[title={Content moderation (Grimmelmann, 2015)}]
``The governance mechanisms that structure participation in a community to facilitate
cooperation and prevent abuse.'' Two parts: \textbf{identification} (when, how) and
\textbf{action} (what is done). Historically manual; scale and speed made automation necessary
as a complement (Usenet spam filters 1993, Wikipedia's AntiVandalBot 2006).
\end{defnbox}

\begin{propbox}[title={Identification: when?}]
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Pre-publication}: reviewed before being published.
  \item \textbf{Post-publication}: systematically, by sampling, or \textbf{reactively} when
        users report it.
\end{itemize}
\end{propbox}

\begin{propbox}[title={Identification: how?}]
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Classification} (put content in a category): natural language processing
        (keyword screening, \textbf{sentiment analysis} of polarity and subjectivity, ML
        classifiers; e.g.\ Twitter's quality filter 2016), computer vision for images and video.
  \item \textbf{Matching} (compare with known content): \textbf{hashing} against a database;
        \textbf{perceptual hashing} is robust to small perturbations (cropping, filters\ldots),
        for text, images, video and audio. Facebook blocked 80\% of the 1.5M re-uploads of the
        Christchurch attack video (2019).
\end{itemize}
\end{propbox}

\begin{exbox}[title={Review question}]
Block uploads that are \emph{slightly modified} versions of copyrighted videos:
\textbf{perceptual hashing} (matching robust to modifications). Sentiment analysis and keyword
screening classify, they do not recognise a known video; shadow banning is an \emph{action}, not
a detection technique.
\end{exbox}

\begin{propbox}[title={Actions (Jiang et al., 2023), from harsh to soft}]
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Exclude}: ban, timeout, read-only (Twitter suspended half a million accounts end
        of 2022).
  \item \textbf{Organize}: delete, annotate, reduce visibility (``shadow banning''); Facebook
        deleted 865.8M posts early 2018.
  \item \textbf{Set norms}: warn.
\end{itemize}
\end{propbox}

\begin{warnbox}[title={Limits of automated moderation (Duarte et al., 2017)}]
Performance depends on the domain; context and nuance (tone, speaker) matter: ``often context and
minor semantic differences separate hate speech from benign speech''; people disagree on
interpretation; users adapt to evade the filters. Human judgement is needed: moderation is
\textbf{hybrid}.
\end{warnbox}

\begin{keybox}[title={The human moderators' dilemma}]
Human moderators face very high mental and physical health risks, with irreversible damage (Meta:
about 15\,000 moderators in 2022, outsourced to more than 20 countries; OpenAI's Kenyan workers
paid under \$2/hour, Perrigo, 2023). We \textbf{knowingly expose some people to high safety risks
to ensure the safety of others}. Content moderation cannot be fully automated.
\end{keybox}

\begin{goatbox}
Moderation = finding bad posts (before or after they are published, by recognising the kind of
post or by matching it with known bad ones) and then doing something (ban, delete, hide, warn).
Machines miss context, so humans help, and those humans get hurt by what they see.
\end{goatbox}


\section{The confusion matrix}

\begin{defnbox}[title={Confusion matrix}]
Compares the output of a system with another source (the ``truth'', e.g.\ human judgement), for
a binary classifier. Rows: \textbf{actual} value; columns: \textbf{predicted} value.
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{l|c|c|}
\multicolumn{1}{c}{} & \multicolumn{2}{c}{\textbf{Predicted}} \\
\multicolumn{1}{c}{} & \multicolumn{1}{c}{Positive} & \multicolumn{1}{c}{Negative} \\ \cline{2-3}
\textbf{Actual} Positive & \textcolor{greenColor}{\textbf{TP}} & \textcolor{redColor}{\textbf{FN}} \\ \cline{2-3}
\textbf{Actual} Negative & \textcolor{redColor}{\textbf{FP}} & \textcolor{greenColor}{\textbf{TN}} \\ \cline{2-3}
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{TP}: predicts positive, correct. \textbf{TN}: predicts negative, correct.
  \item \textbf{FP}: predicts positive, \emph{wrong} (``false alarm'').
        \textbf{FN}: predicts negative, \emph{wrong} (``miss'').
\end{itemize}
The first word (True/False) says if the prediction is right; the second (Positive/Negative) says
\textbf{what was predicted}.
\end{defnbox}

\begin{propbox}[title={Basic metrics}]
\[
\text{Accuracy} = \frac{TP+TN}{TP+TN+FP+FN}
\]
\[
\text{FNR} = \frac{FN}{P} = \frac{FN}{TP+FN}
\qquad
\text{FPR} = \frac{FP}{N} = \frac{FP}{TN+FP}
\]
where $P = TP+FN$ (actual positives) and $N = TN+FP$ (actual negatives).
\end{propbox}

\begin{exbox}[title={Offensive text detector}]
Pairs (system, human): $(+,-), (+,+), (-,-), (+,+), (-,+), (-,+), (-,-)$ give FP, TP, TN, TP,
FN, FN, TN, so $TP=2, FN=2, FP=1, TN=2$. Accuracy $=4/7$, FNR $=2/4=0.5$, FPR $=1/3$.
\end{exbox}

\begin{warnbox}[title={Which error matters depends on the context}]
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Filtering toxic content} (positive = toxic): FP = harmless post censored; FN =
        toxic post escapes.
  \item \textbf{COVID test} (positive = infected): FP $\to$ the patient stays home for nothing;
        FN $\to$ the patient meets others: \textbf{dangerous}.
  \item \textbf{Quality control} where positive = ``good quality'': FP $\to$ a defective part is
        used: \textbf{dangerous}; FN $\to$ more checks. Always check \textbf{what ``positive''
        means}!
\end{itemize}
\end{warnbox}

\begin{exbox}[title={Exam-type questions: fissures in concrete (positive = fissure)}]
\begin{itemize}
  \item TN = actual \emph{absence} of fissure, correct prediction.
  \item For safety, watch the \textbf{FN} (a fissure that is missed); TP also matters (fissures
        properly detected).
  \item Matrix: actual fissure: TP $=60$, FN $=15$; actual no fissure: FP $=20$, TN $=100$.
        $\text{FNR} = \frac{FN}{TP+FN} = \frac{15}{75} = 20\%$.
\end{itemize}
\end{exbox}

\begin{goatbox}
The robot says ``wolf'' or ``no wolf''; reality says wolf or not. Four boxes: right ``wolf''
(TP), right ``no wolf'' (TN), false alarm (FP), missed wolf (FN). A missed wolf eats a goat, a
false alarm just wakes everyone up: which mistake is worse depends on the story.
\end{goatbox}


\section{Strategy: bad actors}

\begin{defnbox}[title={Bad actor (threat actor)}]
A person or group with \textbf{malicious intentions} who will try to exploit a system to achieve
their own goals. Often external, but also \textbf{insiders}; with authorized or unauthorized
access.
\end{defnbox}

\begin{algbox}[title={Applying the strategy (cheat sheet 1.6)}]
\textbf{Goal}: minimize vulnerabilities from the beginning of design through risk assessment
and mitigation. \textbf{When}: design phase, before updates, audits and inspections, incident
response, improving the software. \\
\textbf{Questions}: who would like to use my software maliciously, or in a way I did not create
it for? What would be the consequences for stakeholders? How could I prevent them? \\
Brainstorm with the \textbf{five motivation categories} (they overlap: the goal is a
\emph{range} of threat scenarios, not a perfect classification).
\end{algbox}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{l X X}
\textbf{Motivation} & \textbf{Who} & \textbf{Typical actions} \\ \hline
\textbf{Money} & companies, hackers, scammers, competitors, criminal infrastructure providers
(sell stolen data), big game hunters (study one high-value target), mass scammers (WannaCry
ransomware 2017, $>$300\,000 victims incl.\ the UK NHS) & data theft, ransomware, sabotage by a
competitor, scams \\ \hline
\textbf{Politics} & nation-state actors, intelligence agencies, political candidates (Cozy Bear
/ APT29 and Fancy Bear / APT28 hacked the DNC in 2016) & data disclosure, disinformation,
strategic sabotage, infrastructure attacks \\ \hline
\textbf{Entertainment} & trolls, thrill seekers (LulzSec) & look up a celebrity's medical record,
browse personal photos, attack random systems, spread misinformation \\ \hline
\textbf{Ideas} & extremists, religious groups, hacktivists (Anonymous), terrorists, conspiracy
theorists & spread beliefs, discredit other groups, DoS attacks, data leaks, website defacement
\\ \hline
\textbf{Self-interest} & revenge seekers, self-promoters, obsessive people, insiders (Terry
Childs, San Francisco network admin who refused to give the passwords, convicted 2010) &
harassing messages, monitoring communications, location tracking, grade changing
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\begin{keybox}[title={How the case studies are graded}]
A good description: (1) describes a \textbf{threat} with its \textbf{intentional} nature made
clear; (2) makes the link to the \textbf{motivation category} explicit; (3) gives the
\textbf{causal chain} action $\to$ impact(s) on stakeholders; (4) is specific to the scenario;
(5) the actions are diverse (types of threat, types of harm). Example: ``\emph{Money}: scammers
create fake, trustworthy-looking profiles to trick people into giving money or payment details,
leading to financial loss for the victims.'' Counter-example: an outage (no intent, so not a bad
actor).
\end{keybox}

\begin{goatbox}
Imagine the villains: who wants to misuse your app, and why (money, politics, fun, ideas,
revenge)? For each one, say what they do and who gets hurt. Then you can lock those doors.
\end{goatbox}


\section{Strategy: STRIDE}

\begin{defnbox}[title={STRIDE (Microsoft)}]
A \textbf{threat modelling} strategy from cybersecurity: a mnemonic of six categories of attacks.
Each category is a typical attack to anticipate systematically, and the violation of a security
property. A complement to bad actors, usable at any stage. (Others: MISUSE for threats to
individuals, DREAD to rate risks.)
\begin{center}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{l X l}
\textbf{Threat} & \textbf{What happens} & \textbf{Property violated} \\ \hline
\textbf{S}poofing & a person or program pretends to be someone or something else & Authenticity \\
\textbf{T}ampering & covertly modifying components (logic or data) & Integrity \\
\textbf{R}epudiation & someone denies an action and nothing can prove them wrong (no tracking) & Accountability \\
\textbf{I}nformation disclosure & information exposed to people not supposed to see it & Confidentiality \\
\textbf{D}enial of service & disrupting a service or making it unavailable by overloading it & Availability \\
\textbf{E}levation of privilege & obtaining higher privileges for unauthorized actions & Authorization
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
\end{defnbox}

\begin{keybox}
Goal: identify vulnerabilities by analysing typical attack patterns of bad actors
(\textbf{security}) that can result in harm for stakeholders (\textbf{safety}). For each threat,
propose a countermeasure (authentication, signatures and logs, encryption, rate limiting,
least privilege\ldots).
\end{keybox}

\begin{goatbox}
STRIDE is a checklist of six tricks of a burglar: wear a disguise (S), change things in secret
(T), say ``it wasn't me'' (R), peek at secrets (I), block the door (D), steal the master key (E).
\end{goatbox}


\section{Strategy: harm modeling}

\begin{rembox}[title={Why?}]
Good intentions are not enough, and adoption is fast: 1M users took Netflix about 3.5 years,
Spotify about 5 months, ChatGPT about 5 days.
\end{rembox}

\begin{algbox}[title={Applying the strategy (cheat sheet 1.7)}]
\textbf{Goal}: anticipate the harms users and stakeholders may encounter, and address them
\textbf{iteratively} until the potential for harm is satisfactory. \textbf{When}: at every stage,
for every feature if possible. \textbf{Question}: how could my tech harm users and other
stakeholders? Three elements:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item the \textbf{category of use}: malfunction, abuse, misuse, intended use;
  \item the \textbf{types of harm} (taxonomy below): brainstorm examples;
  \item the \textbf{magnitude} of each harm: \textbf{severity} (how acutely), \textbf{scale}
        (how broadly across populations), \textbf{probability} (how likely), \textbf{frequency}
        (how often); e.g.\ low / moderate / high.
\end{enumerate}
\end{algbox}

\begin{defnbox}[title={Taxonomy of harms (adapted from Microsoft): \emph{what} is harmed}]
\begin{center}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{l l X}
\textbf{Category} & \textbf{Type} & \textbf{Meaning and examples} \\ \hline
\textbf{Humans} & Physical injury & hurt people or create dangerous environments (failures of
physical systems, health, incitement to violence) \\
 & Emotional / psychological injury & severe distress (identity, reputation, mental health,
addiction) \\ \hline
\textbf{Allocation} & Opportunity loss & automated decisions limit access to resources, services,
opportunities (employment, insurance, housing, education) \\
\textbf{of resources} & Economic loss & decisions on financial instruments amplify inequities
(differential pricing, credit, exploitation) \\ \hline
\textbf{Human rights} & Dignity loss & how people perceive and value one another (public shaming,
humiliation, dehumanization) \\
 & Liberty loss & automating legal, judicial, social systems (policing errors, crowd control,
consent, free speech) \\
 & Privacy loss & facts or assumptions about someone without their knowledge (tracking, forced
data collection) \\
 & Environmental impact & every decision of the life cycle (emissions, resources, e-waste) \\
\hline
\textbf{Social systems} & Manipulation & personalized manipulative experiences undermine an
informed citizenry and trust (disinformation, deception) \\
 & Social detriment & effects on communities and society (deskilling, stereotype reinforcement,
polarization, conflicts)
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
Not all categories apply to each case; some harms fit several; adapt to the project. (Shelby
et al., 2023, propose another taxonomy focused on consequences, without physical harm.)
\end{defnbox}

\begin{exbox}[title={Review questions: which harm?}]
\begin{itemize}
  \item A user's post is unfairly censored $\to$ \textbf{liberty loss} (free speech).
  \item A fitness app leaks GPS locations on social media $\to$ \textbf{privacy loss}.
  \item A recruitment software indirectly discriminates based on names $\to$
        \textbf{opportunity loss}.
  \item Image search for ``nurse'' shows only women $\to$ \textbf{social detriment}
        (stereotype reinforcement).
  \item Online ads push a compulsive shopper to buy more $\to$ ambiguous: psychological injury,
        economic loss, manipulation (but manipulation targets social systems, here an
        individual). Argue your choice.
\end{itemize}
Method: first \emph{what} is harmed (category), then the \emph{type} in that category.
\end{exbox}

\begin{keybox}[title={How the case studies are graded}]
The description (1) describes a \textbf{harm} (a negative impact on stakeholders); (2) makes
clear \textbf{how the software leads to it}; (3) makes the link to the harm category explicit;
(4) is specific to the scenario. Do it for several scenarios, including \emph{not} creating the
product. It combines well with stakeholder analysis and the four scenarios of harm.
\end{keybox}

\begin{goatbox}
Go through a list of ``things that can get hurt'' (bodies, minds, money and chances, rights,
society) and ask for each: can my app hurt this? How badly, how many, how likely, how often?
\end{goatbox}


\begin{recapbox}[title={Exam-ready recap --- Safety 1}]
\textbf{Safety vs.\ security}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Security}: environment $\to$ system, \textbf{threats}, \textbf{intentional}.
        \textbf{Safety}: system $\to$ environment, \textbf{hazards}, \textbf{accidental}. Risk =
        probability of something bad. One event can be both (stickers on a stop sign).
  \item Software is a \textbf{socio-technical system}: hidden loops, emergence.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Four scenarios of harm}: \textbf{malfunction} (bug), \textbf{abuse} (malicious intent),
\textbf{misuse} (unintended use, no malice), \textbf{intended use} (works as planned, still
harms: the hardest).

\textbf{UGC and moderation}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Harms of UGC: hate \& harassment, self-inflicted harm, ideological harm, exploitation.
        Hate speech: discriminatory + identity factor + any form of expression.
  \item Free speech (UDHR art.\ 19) is not absolute. Dilemma: \textbf{FN} = toxic content
        escapes vs.\ \textbf{FP} = harmless content censored.
  \item Moderation = identification (\emph{pre} / \emph{post}-publication, reactive on reports;
        \textbf{classification}: keywords, sentiment, ML, vision; \textbf{matching}: hashing,
        \textbf{perceptual hashing} for modified copies) + action (\textbf{exclude} $>$
        \textbf{organize} $>$ \textbf{set norms}).
  \item Automation misses context $\Rightarrow$ hybrid; human moderators bear severe risks
        (a dilemma).
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Confusion matrix} (rows actual, columns predicted): $\text{Acc}=\frac{TP+TN}{\text{all}}$,
$\text{FNR}=\frac{FN}{TP+FN}$, $\text{FPR}=\frac{FP}{TN+FP}$. Always check what
\emph{positive} means; which error is dangerous depends on the context.

\textbf{Strategies}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Bad actors}: 5 motivations \textbf{Money, Politics, Entertainment, Ideas,
        Self-interest}. Describe: intent + motivation + action $\to$ impact on stakeholders.
  \item \textbf{STRIDE}: Spoofing/authenticity, Tampering/integrity,
        Repudiation/accountability, Information disclosure/confidentiality, Denial of
        service/availability, Elevation of privilege/authorization.
  \item \textbf{Harm modeling}: category of use + type of harm + magnitude (severity, scale,
        probability, frequency). 4 categories / 10 types: Humans (physical, emotional),
        Allocation (opportunity, economic), Human rights (dignity, liberty, privacy,
        environment), Social systems (manipulation, social detriment).
\end{itemize}
\end{recapbox}

\section{From micro to macro}

\begin{defnbox}[title={Three levels of analysis}]
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Micro}: individuals.
  \item \textbf{Meso}: groups, communities, organisations.
  \item \textbf{Macro}: societies, economies, the planet.
\end{itemize}
Macro effects come from usage \textbf{by many}, \textbf{in many places}, \textbf{a lot / all the
time}, \textbf{for a long time}.
\end{defnbox}

\begin{exbox}[title={The internet as a macro-scale system}]
Layers (Jones, 2014; Flensburg \& Lai, 2020): devices and access network, backbone, applications,
content. About 600 submarine cables carry about 99\% of all traffic (1.4M km in total, the
longest SEA-ME-WE 3 about 39\,000 km); about 4.7 billion users in 2020; 1.2 zettabytes
($1.2\cdot 10^{21}$ bytes) of IP traffic in 2016.
\end{exbox}

\begin{propbox}[title={A double-edged sword (Brey, 2019)}]
The internet brings information access, communication, social relations and communities,
production and commerce; but also harms to private/public boundaries and relationships,
addiction, harm to cognitive development, information overload, harmful effects on knowledge and
belief, and harm to democracy and citizenship.
\begin{itemize}
  \item Macro impacts: on large numbers of individuals, on social structures and institutions,
        different for different groups. Areas: markets and economy, knowledge and worldviews,
        health and well-being, politics and citizenship, environment.
  \item They are \textbf{controversial}: hard to measure, causality hard to establish.
  \item Example: LLMs as a threat to science through hallucinations (Mittelstadt et al., 2023).
\end{itemize}
\end{propbox}

\begin{keybox}[title={The ``problem of one person'' (microethics vs.\ macroethics)}]
One engineer alone cannot solve macro problems (Herkert, 2005; Conlon \& Zandvoort, 2011; Gogoll
et al., 2021). What one can do: involve stakeholders at micro, meso and macro levels in design
decisions; work with experts of the social sciences and humanities; choose which companies to
work for (business model, management style, ethical culture).
\end{keybox}

\begin{exbox}[title={Review question: GenAI and learning}]
Potentially all levels, but it has all the characteristics of \textbf{macro} (many people,
everywhere, all the time, for years). A study (Stromberg et al., 2026) of 26\,811 Chinese
students over 30 months: with AI adoption, homework scores $+18\%$ and completion time $-30\%$,
but monthly closed-book exam scores $-20\%$ within 6 months; losses especially large for junior
students and those who \textbf{outsource} (80\% of AI users). Users who kept their completion
time had a small learning loss.
\end{exbox}

\begin{goatbox}
One goat eating one flower: no problem (micro). The whole herd eating flowers every day for
years: no more meadow (macro). Some software problems only appear when everybody uses it, all
the time, for a long time.
\end{goatbox}


\section{Dis- and mis-information}

\begin{defnbox}[title={Types of false information (Zhou \& Zafarani, 2020; Pennycook \& Rand, 2021)}]
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{l|c|c}
 & \textbf{False?} & \textbf{Intent to deceive / harm?} \\ \hline
\textbf{Misinformation}  & yes & no \\
\textbf{Disinformation}  & yes & yes \\
\textbf{Malinformation}  & no (true) & yes (to harm) \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\textbf{Fake news} = false information \textbf{in the style of news} (a subset defined by its
style).
\end{defnbox}

\begin{exbox}[title={Exam-type question}]
A good friend tells you ``eating carrots drastically improves your night vision'': your friend
believes it, so it is \textbf{misinformation} (it was disinformation by the British in World War
II, hiding their radar).
\end{exbox}

\begin{propbox}[title={Why do we believe false information? (Ecker et al., 2022)}]
\textbf{Cognitive drivers}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{System 1} (fast, automatic, emotional, unconscious) vs.\ \textbf{System 2}
        (slow, effortful, logical, conscious).
  \item \textbf{Intuitive thinking}: relying on mental shortcuts: familiarity, ``gut feeling''.
  \item \textbf{Illusory truth effect}: \textbf{repetition} makes claims more believable (true or
        false, even implausible): more familiarity, less effort to process, impression of
        coherence.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Socio-affective drivers}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Source cues}: credibility of the source (attractiveness, power, similarity),
        in-group preference, \textbf{false-consensus effect} (overestimating how many people
        share your belief).
  \item \textbf{Emotions}: those conveyed by the content and the receiver's emotional state.
  \item \textbf{Worldview}: identity, values, partisanship.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Resistance to correction}: corrections (debunking, prebunking) are weakened by the
\textbf{continued influence effect}: false information keeps influencing reasoning after being
corrected (Lewandowsky et al., 2012).
\end{propbox}

\begin{exbox}[title={Exam-type questions}]
\begin{itemize}
  \item You still believe aliens made crop circles weeks after reading the debunk $\to$
        \textbf{continued influence}.
  \item A post by Elon Musk with false claims about US elections: you are likely to believe it
        because of \textbf{source cues} (power, popularity of the source).
  \item One emotionally charged word (``fight'') in political tweets boosts reposts by 20\% $\to$
        \textbf{genuine resharing through intuitive reactions} (the recommender only amplifies
        afterwards).
\end{itemize}
\end{exbox}

\begin{keybox}[title={Why it matters at macro level}]
The World Economic Forum's Global Risks Report 2024 ranks mis- and disinformation as the
\textbf{most severe global risk for the next 2 years}: disruption of elections, distrust of
information, repression and erosion of rights. Digital media use correlates with more exposure to
misinformation, more polarization, less trust (e.g.\ in vaccines), more hate; but the size of the
exposure, the role of users vs.\ algorithms and the causal role of social media are debated
(Lorenz-Spreen et al., 2023; Budak et al., 2024).
\end{keybox}

\begin{goatbox}
Misinformation: a goat repeats a false rumour it believes. Disinformation: a fox invents the
rumour on purpose. Malinformation: the fox tells a true secret to hurt you. We believe rumours
because we think fast, we hear them again and again, we trust those who say them, they make us
feel things, and once believed they are hard to unlearn.
\end{goatbox}


\section{Software and dis-/mis-information}

\begin{defnbox}[title={The supply chain of false information (Zhou \& Zafarani, 2020)}]
\textbf{Creation} $\longrightarrow$ \textbf{Publication} $\longrightarrow$ \textbf{Propagation}
\end{defnbox}

\begin{propbox}[title={Creation}]
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Content farms}: humans paid to create large quantities of content, for
        advertising revenue (e.g.\ Demand Media) or a political / ideological agenda.
  \item \textbf{Generative AI and deepfakes}: easy production in volume and speed; multimodal,
        realistic, personalized, persuasive.
\end{itemize}
\end{propbox}

\begin{propbox}[title={Publication}]
Real users (influential figures and ordinary people; financial interests with clickbait;
professional groups running coordinated campaigns, i.e.\ trolls), fake accounts, troll farms,
\textbf{social bots} (automated publication: 1--15\% of accounts but a disproportionate share of
the content).
\end{propbox}

\begin{propbox}[title={Propagation}]
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Humans}: reposting and liking. A few \emph{supersharers} repost a lot (bans
        reduce reposting); click farms are paid to inflate engagement; genuine resharing comes
        from inattention, emotions (intuitive reactions) and socio-affective reasons (group
        membership, self-promotion, sparking emotions such as outrage in others).
  \item \textbf{Software}: social bots repost early after the first publication, reply to or
        mention real accounts with large networks, target ``opponents''; \textbf{recommender
        systems} select what we see, optimizing \textbf{engagement}, with a \textbf{popularity
        bias}.
\end{itemize}
\end{propbox}

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{Humans} (bad actors with political or financial motives, but also regular users, as
producers and receivers) and \textbf{software} (tools and platform algorithms) form a
\textbf{socio-technical system with hidden feedback loops} (Borge-Holthoefer et al., 2013).
Humans spread disinformation because it is their work, by inattention, for political reasons, to
please others, to spark emotions: all of these.
\end{keybox}

\begin{goatbox}
A lie is made (by people or by AI), posted (by people, fake accounts or robots), then spread (by
people who like and share it, and by the app's algorithm that pushes what gets clicks). Each step
helps the next: a loop.
\end{goatbox}


\section{Strategy: edge cases}

\begin{defnbox}[title={Edge case}]
In software testing, a scenario at the \textbf{extreme ends or boundaries} of operating
conditions (``what if\ldots'' the parameter of a division is 0?). Here the idea is
``hijacked'' to anticipate \textbf{macro-level} effects. Premise: the product is deployed.
\end{defnbox}

\begin{algbox}[title={Applying the strategy (cheat sheet 2.6)}]
\textbf{Goal}: anticipate how the product behaves in extreme conditions, to find improvements
that prevent unintended consequences for stakeholders. \textbf{When}: every stage, before major
updates, as the user base grows, when the target audience changes. Three cases:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Global Reach} (focus on \textbf{differences}): what happens if people from
        different countries and backgrounds use it? Human diversity (languages, cultures and
        beliefs, knowledge and education); structural differences (regulations, energy and
        network, time zones).
  \item \textbf{Mass Adoption} (focus on \textbf{scale}): what if an unexpectedly huge number of
        people use it? Scalability (infrastructure, performance under load, security,
        reliability); social impact (large-scale effects, communities, social norms, societal
        structures and democracy).
  \item \textbf{Longevity} (focus on \textbf{time}): what if it is used over a long period?
        Long-term use (uninterrupted operation, prolonged use, usage changes and feature
        evolution); future impact (societal changes, obsolescence and end of life).
\end{itemize}
Helpful tools: the Tarot Cards of Tech, the Envisioning Cards.
\end{algbox}

\begin{keybox}[title={How the case studies are graded}]
Describe an \textbf{extreme scenario} that creates a challenge for the product \textbf{and} its
impact on stakeholders, with the link to the category explicit. Examples (video meeting app):
\emph{Longevity}: language changes over time, so the transcription must adapt or produce wrong
transcripts. \emph{Global Reach}: advanced cameras are too expensive for many, so some people
cannot use the product. Then propose a \textbf{mitigation} by changing the design.
\end{keybox}

\begin{goatbox}
Push your app to the extremes: used by everybody everywhere (different languages, laws,
internet), by way too many people at once, for 20 years. What breaks, and who suffers?
\end{goatbox}


\section{Strategy: the Ethics Canvas}

\begin{defnbox}[title={The Ethics Canvas (Reijers et al., 2018)}]
A tool to ease, structure and communicate the analysis of ethical impacts, made by the ADAPT
Centre (Dublin), inspired by the Business Model Canvas (Osterwalder \& Pigneur, 2010). A
\emph{canvas} is a ``visual tool designed to guide people through the process of using a
methodology or framework'' (Hardebolle et al., 2023). \textbf{Bottom-up}, \textbf{holistic}
(the social and technical systems affect each other), ideal early in development. Goals:
identify ``group'' stakeholders and wide ethical impacts.
\end{defnbox}

\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{images/week05_01.png}
\end{center}

\begin{algbox}[title={Nine blocks, three stages (cheat sheet 2.4)}]
\textbf{Stage 1 --- identify stakeholders} (\emph{who might be affected?})
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Individuals affected}: who uses it, who is affected, their characteristics
        (\emph{micro}).
  \item \textbf{Groups affected}: \emph{organised} groups involved in design, production,
        distribution, use, or affected: businesses, communities, institutions, interest groups,
        governments (\emph{meso / macro}).
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Stage 2 --- identify ethical impacts} (\emph{what potential impacts?})
\begin{enumerate}\setcounter{enumi}{2}
  \item \textbf{Behaviour}: habits, time schedules, choice of activities (micro).
  \item \textbf{Relations}: between friends, family, co-workers (micro).
  \item \textbf{Worldviews}: ideas about consumption, culture, religion, work (macro).
  \item \textbf{Group conflicts}: discrimination, people put out of work (macro).
  \item \textbf{Product or service failure}: technical errors, security failures, use not as
        intended.
  \item \textbf{Problematic use of resources}: energy, personal data.
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Stage 3 --- remedial actions} (\emph{how to address them?})
\begin{enumerate}\setcounter{enumi}{8}
  \item \textbf{What can we do?} The most important impacts and how to address them (design,
        organisation, broader changes).
\end{enumerate}
\end{algbox}

\begin{exbox}[title={An image search engine (Guilbeault et al., 2024: online images amplify gender bias)}]
Groups: the developer team, the company, minorities and discriminated groups, ethnic minorities,
women. Worldviews: specific jobs and social roles shown for specific groups $\to$ a stereotyped
view of society. Group conflicts: majority vs.\ minorities.
\end{exbox}

\begin{warnbox}[title={Common difficulty}]
Do not mix block 1 (\textbf{types of individuals}, micro) and block 2 (\textbf{organised groups}:
businesses, communities, institutions, governments; meso / macro).
\end{warnbox}

\begin{goatbox}
A big sheet with nine boxes: who is touched (single goats, organised herds), what changes for
them (habits, friendships, ideas, fights between herds, breakdowns, wasted resources), and what
we do about it.
\end{goatbox}


\section{Strategy: systems thinking and causal loop diagrams}

\begin{defnbox}[title={System and systems thinking (Kauffman, 1980; Meadows, 2008; Crowley, 2022)}]
A \textbf{system} is a collection of organised \textbf{parts} which interact with each other to
function as a \textbf{whole} to reach a state or purpose. \textbf{Systems thinking} is the skill
to simplify complexity, identify patterns, explain the system's behaviour and create solutions,
by modelling the parts, their relationships and the dynamics of the whole. Tools: equations and
simulations, diagrams (\textbf{causal loop diagrams}, stock and flow diagrams).
\end{defnbox}

\begin{defnbox}[title={Elements of a causal loop diagram (cheat sheet 2.5)}]
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Aim, focus and boundaries} of the analysis.
  \item \textbf{Key variables}: a quantity that changes over time; a short label, \textbf{no
        verb} (``Fake news quantity'', ``Auto.\ detection investment'', ``Human detection
        skills'').
  \item \textbf{Causal links}: arrow $A \to B$ with
        \textbf{+} (or S, \emph{same}): $A$ up $\Rightarrow$ $B$ up, and $A$ down $\Rightarrow$
        $B$ down; \textbf{$-$} (or O, \emph{opposite}): $A$ up $\Rightarrow$ $B$ down, and $A$
        down $\Rightarrow$ $B$ up.
  \item \textbf{Delays}: a long arrow or a double bar \textbf{//} on the arrow: the effect comes
        after some time.
  \item \textbf{Feedback loops}: a closed chain of links.
\end{itemize}
\end{defnbox}

\begin{defnbox}[title={Balancing and reinforcing loops}]
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Balancing loop (B)}: counteracts change and brings the system back to an
        equilibrium: it \textbf{reaches a plateau}.
  \item \textbf{Reinforcing loop (R)}: amplifies change: \textbf{growth or collapse}.
\end{itemize}
Rule: count the $-$ links of the loop: \textbf{even} number (0, 2, \ldots) $\Rightarrow$
\textbf{reinforcing}; \textbf{odd} $\Rightarrow$ \textbf{balancing}.
\end{defnbox}

\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=0.48\linewidth]{images/week05_02.png}\hfill
\includegraphics[width=0.48\linewidth]{images/week05_03.png}
\end{center}

\begin{exbox}[title={Fake news (left: balancing, right: reinforcing)}]
\textbf{Left}: more fake news $\xrightarrow{+}$ more investment in automatic detection
$\xrightarrow{-}$ less fake news $\xrightarrow{+}$ less investment\ldots: one $-$, so
\textbf{balancing}: it stabilizes. \\
\textbf{Right}: more fake news $\xrightarrow{+}$ more automatic detection $\xrightarrow{-}$ fewer
human detection skills (people rely on the machine) $\xrightarrow{-}$ more fake news: two $-$, so
\textbf{reinforcing}: it keeps growing.
\end{exbox}

\begin{exbox}[title={Review questions of the session}]
\begin{itemize}
  \item The arrow labelled ``+'' from X to Y means: \textbf{Y changes in the same direction as X}
        (so X up $\Rightarrow$ Y up \textbf{and} X down $\Rightarrow$ Y down).
  \item In a reinforcing loop, whether quantities increase or decrease \textbf{depends} on the
        first variable to change.
  \item \textbf{Predictive policing}: more police in a zone $\to$ more \emph{observed} crime
        there $\to$ more data $\to$ the system sends more police there: reinforcing. Since more
        data was historically collected in zone A, it leads to \textbf{over-policing in A}
        (observed crime, not true crime). The EU Fundamental Rights Agency (2022) showed such
        feedback loops with simulations.
\end{itemize}
\end{exbox}

\begin{goatbox}
Draw the important quantities as circles and arrows between them: ``+'' if they move together,
``$-$'' if they move opposite. Follow a loop: if it pushes back (odd number of $-$), things calm
down; if it pushes along (even number of $-$), things snowball.
\end{goatbox}


\begin{recapbox}[title={Exam-ready recap --- Safety 2}]
\textbf{Levels}: \textbf{micro} (individuals), \textbf{meso} (groups, organisations),
\textbf{macro} (society, economy, planet). Macro = used by many, in many places, all the time,
for long. Macro impacts are real but hard to measure and to prove causally; one engineer alone
cannot fix them (involve stakeholders, social sciences, choose your employer).

\textbf{False information}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Mis}- = false, no intent. \textbf{Dis}- = false, with intent.
        \textbf{Mal}- = true, used to harm. Fake news = in the style of news.
  \item Belief drivers: cognitive (\textbf{System 1}, intuitive thinking, \textbf{illusory truth}
        = repetition); socio-affective (\textbf{source cues}, \textbf{false consensus},
        emotions, worldview). Correction resisted: \textbf{continued influence effect}.
  \item Supply chain: \textbf{creation} (content farms, GenAI / deepfakes) $\to$
        \textbf{publication} (users, fake accounts, troll farms, social bots) $\to$
        \textbf{propagation} (resharing, supersharers, click farms, bots, \textbf{recommender
        systems}: engagement, popularity bias). A socio-technical system with feedback loops.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Strategies}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Edge cases}: \textbf{Global Reach} (differences), \textbf{Mass Adoption} (scale),
        \textbf{Longevity} (time). Extreme scenario + impact on stakeholders + mitigation.
  \item \textbf{Ethics Canvas}: 9 blocks / 3 stages. 1 individuals, 2 groups; 3 behaviour,
        4 relations, 5 worldviews, 6 group conflicts, 7 failure, 8 resources; 9 what can we do.
  \item \textbf{Causal loop diagrams}: variables (nouns), links $+$ (same direction) / $-$
        (opposite), delays (//). Loop with an \textbf{odd} number of $-$ = \textbf{balancing}
        (plateau); \textbf{even} = \textbf{reinforcing} (growth or collapse).
\end{itemize}
\end{recapbox}

\begin{rembox}[title={In a nutshell}]
\textbf{What is this chapter about?} How data about people raises legal and ethical issues
(sensitive and proxy attributes, data is not objective), what fairness and bias are, where bias
comes from (algorithms, data, people), and two strategies: \textbf{inclusive design} and
\textbf{value analysis}. \\
\textbf{Why is it useful?} Unfair software almost always comes from a bias somewhere in the
process; naming the bias is the first step to fix it.
\end{rembox}


\section{Data and people}

\begin{rembox}[title={Where software works with data about people}]
Health, human resources, retail, leisure and sports, education, media, banking and insurance,
government; less obvious: smart cities, autonomous vehicles, the Internet of Things. Representing
people with data (\textbf{attributes}) raises two concerns: \textbf{confidentiality / privacy}
and \textbf{fairness / non-discrimination}. Running example: an app that asks for your
\emph{height} (shopping app, dating app).
\end{rembox}

\begin{defnbox}[title={Sensitive attributes}]
Data about people that may have \textbf{legal or ethical implications}: they are
\textbf{legally} sensitive or \textbf{ethically} sensitive.
\end{defnbox}

\begin{defnbox}[title={Legally sensitive (van Bekkum \& Zuiderveen Borgesius, 2023)}]
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Data protection law} (e.g.\ GDPR): \textbf{personal data} reveals information
        about the \emph{identity} of people, directly or indirectly; \textbf{special category
        data} is \textbf{banned by default}: racial or ethnic origin, political opinions,
        religious beliefs, health data\ldots
  \item \textbf{Non-discrimination law}: forbids \textbf{direct discrimination} and
        \textbf{indirect discrimination} = an \emph{apparently neutral} practice that puts people
        from specific groups at a particular disadvantage. \textbf{Protected group / class}: a
        category qualifying for special protection by law or policy.
\end{itemize}
Example: Google's ad system showed high-income jobs 6 times more to men than to women (Datta et
al., 2015). \emph{Age} is personal data (not special category) and explicitly named in
non-discrimination law: legally sensitive.
\end{defnbox}

\begin{defnbox}[title={Ethically sensitive (Imtiaz, 2022)}]
\emph{Height} could reveal identity (especially combined with other data), could be seen as
health data, is not named in non-discrimination law, \textbf{but can lead to discrimination}
(``heightism'' in careers): ethically sensitive.
\end{defnbox}

\begin{defnbox}[title={Proxy attributes}]
Variables that \textbf{correlate with sensitive attributes} (height correlates with age, gender,
health). They can lead to \textbf{indirect discrimination that is harder to detect}.
\end{defnbox}

\begin{exbox}[title={Exam-type question}]
A loan recommender uses SAT scores, which correlate with race: the SAT score is a \textbf{proxy
attribute}.
\end{exbox}

\begin{defnbox}[title={Simplifying reality (Jacobs \& Wallach, 2021)}]
Height is \textbf{observable} and \textbf{measurable}. But often we want information that is
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{difficult or impossible to obtain}: \emph{arrests} used to represent \emph{crime};
  \item \textbf{not directly observable}: website visits and clicks used to represent students'
        \emph{interest}.
\end{itemize}
An \textbf{observed variable} stands for a \textbf{latent variable} (a construct).
\end{defnbox}

\begin{keybox}[title={Data $\neq$ objectivity (boyd \& Crawford, 2012; Feinberg, 2023)}]
Data is an \textbf{imperfect representation of the real world}: simplifications and
\textbf{choices} made with a specific goal. ``The design decisions that determine what will be
measured also stem from interpretation \ldots\ This process is inherently subjective.''
\end{keybox}

\begin{exbox}[title={Review question: hair colour as an attribute}]
Not legally sensitive; it is \textbf{observed}; and \textbf{subjective} (``red'' for one person
is ``auburn'' for another; categories must be predefined and some people fit none, as with skin
colour). Only measuring it by colorimetry would make it objective.
\end{exbox}

\begin{goatbox}
When an app stores facts about you, some facts are protected by law (religion, health, age),
some are not but can still be used against you (height), and some innocent-looking facts secretly
reveal protected ones (proxies). And data is never the full truth: someone chose what to measure.
\end{goatbox}


\section{Sources of unfairness}

\begin{defnbox}[title={Fairness: three questions (Gosepath, 2021; Binns, 2018)}]
Fairness is related to \textbf{equality}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item equality of \textbf{what}? Resources, opportunities\ldots
  \item \textbf{how}? Treating everyone the same (\emph{equality}), or considering individual
        circumstances beyond one's choice (\emph{equity});
  \item for \textbf{whom}?
\end{itemize}
\end{defnbox}

\begin{defnbox}[title={Bias}]
A \textbf{systematic deviation from a standard}. It is \textbf{relative} (to a standard) and
\textbf{not necessarily bad}, but it can lead to ethical issues, e.g.\ unfairness. In a
software-based process, \textbf{bias} produces the effect \textbf{unfairness}:
\textbf{discrimination}, \textbf{errors}, \textbf{stereotyping}. Bias is found in
\textbf{algorithms}, \textbf{data} and \textbf{people}.
\end{defnbox}
